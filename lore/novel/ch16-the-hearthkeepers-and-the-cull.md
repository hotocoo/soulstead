# Chapter 16: The Hearthkeepers and the Cull

> *A Husk is a person who was failed. What it is owed is a rite. It is not owed a blade.*
> Recorded: Wren Ashfall, Healer, at the Hearth on the twenty-fourth, in Bran Holt's second hand

**Hand:** Bran Holt, second hand, with four villager accounts and two I have set aside
**Days:** 24
**Place:** the west field; then the Hearth

The barley in the west field was ready on the twenty-fourth and there was a thing standing
in it.

That is the whole of the day. Everything after this is the Hold of Ashfall arguing about a
sentence with two halves in it, and the argument ran a fortnight, and it has not stopped
in two hundred days, and I am not going to stop it here.

Bran Holt's entry for the twenty-fourth is nine words and a verb he did not like. I have
put it at the foot of this chapter where the board's own words go, and I will say now, so
that nobody has to wait for it, that the verb has the word for an enemy in it, and that
the subject of it is a woman who chose this ground and told these people where to put the
Hearth.

·  ·  ·

Oda Ashfall was buried whole on the ninth day with her knife in the grave, and the thing
that had been walking the west field went down while everybody watched, mid-motion, in the
middle of a stroke it had made about four thousand times.

That is established. Sila Carver cut it. Twenty-two people were standing on the headland.
Every one of them felt the spending get cheaper that afternoon and the Hold got more work
done before dark than it had managed in the four days before, and that is on the board as
the first Vigil in the Vale.

And on the twenty-fourth day there was a thing in the west field walking the reaping
motion.

No two accounts agree about how that is possible. I have four. Two of them are versions of
the other two and I have left them out. Here are the two.

Bran Holt's account is a tally-keeper's account and it is the better of them for being
willing to be small. He wrote that a Keeping takes what a Keeping can reach. Sila Carver
had dug the hole on a reasoning she made up in an afternoon, which was that what the grip
had closed around should go into the ground with it, and she put in the woman and the
woman's knife because the knife was the part she could pick up. Bran Holt's point is that
Oda Ashfall was eleven hours dying and was awake for most of them, and that what she spent
those eleven hours doing was giving instructions about work. She asked twice whether the
stook of stakes had been set upright. She said the lye vat wanted stirring before the skin
set on the top of it. She said that the west field wanted cutting before the wind turned.

A grip that has closed around a sentence cannot be put in a hole. That is Bran Holt's
whole argument and he wrote it in one line and then wrote the weather.

Sable Kettle's account is the other one, and Colm Grey held it too for reasons of his own
that had nothing to do with hers. Sable Kettle was fifteen years old on the twenty-fourth
and had been the Hold's Forager since the third day because she had put her thumb in the
ground twice and told them the truth about it both times. Her account is that there is a
second body in the west field and nobody has ever looked for it.

She had grounds. There is an old byre a hundred paces down the slope, roofless, standing
shoulder height, and the Hold took the stone off its walls in the first afternoon and
carried it up on a hurdle, and there was animal bone in the floor of it, and nobody in the
Hold has ever spoken about that bone in public since. Somebody worked the west field before
the Ashfall family came up that rise and put a knotted cord across it. Sable Kettle's
position is that the reaping motion is not Oda Ashfall's motion. It is the field's motion.
It is what anybody has ever done in that field, and what is standing up in it is whatever
the ninth day did not pay for, wearing the only shape that ground knows.

I record what was said. I do not settle it.

Wren Ashfall would not hold either account. She was the Hold's Healer and she was Oda
Ashfall's younger sister and she had had both hands in her sister's leg for eleven hours
on the sixth day, and when Bran Holt put the question to her at the Hearth she said she
did not care how it was possible. She said the only thing she was certain of was that
whatever was in the west field was something the Hold owed, and that she would rather be
wrong about the mechanism than right about the debt.

That is the Healers' position stated whole for the first time in the Vale, and it was
stated by a woman who was refusing to speculate, and every Healer in this valley has stood
on it since.

·  ·  ·

The reason the twenty-fourth was the day and not some other day is that the barley was
ready.

Ketil Dunn said so at the fire on the twenty-third. He had farmed for thirty years before
he came into the Vale and he had stood in the middle of the first Wrenfield harvest with
his hands on his knees saying he had been doing it wrong his whole life, and when a man
like that says the ears are going over, the ears are going over. Ash-grown barley in its
second season comes up so thick you cannot see soil between the rows, and the straw is
soft and pale and too long, so it goes over in wind. You have four or five days. You do not
have nine.

Bran Holt costed it out loud, because that is what he did. The west field was a fifth of
what the Hold was going to eat. Cutting it took eleven people two days. Not cutting it took
nothing and cost a fifth.

And there was a thing standing in it, and the thing had twenty-two of reach, and it walked
at thirty-four, and it gave you forty-five hundredths of a second between deciding and
arriving.

Forty-five hundredths is the number the Wardens give for it and they give it with a kind of
disgust. A quarter of a second is a noise something makes on its way. Forty-five hundredths
is long enough to watch. It is long enough to see the shoulders load and the hips come
round and the arms start the stroke, and it is long enough, standing at twenty-two of
reach, to recognise the stroke, and the recognising is the whole of the horror and every
Warden in this Vale will tell you so.

Oda Ashfall reaped left to right and finished high because of an old trouble in her right
shoulder. Eleven people in the west field knew that stroke by sight.

·  ·  ·

The Hearth on the twenty-fourth was the first time the Hold argued doctrine instead of
arguing work, and I have had to reconstruct it out of four accounts because Bran Holt
recorded the decision and the numbers and nine words, and did not record one sentence that
was said, because he had not been asked to.

I resent him for it. I have resented him for forty years and I have copied him out
faithfully every time, and those two facts sit next to each other in me the way they sit
next to each other in this Chronicle.

Wren Ashfall spoke first because Bran Holt asked her first, and he asked her first because
she was the Healer and the question had a body in it.

She said what is at the head of this chapter. Then she said the rest of it, and the rest of
it is a rota and not a sermon, which is why it won.

She said: take the crop. Cut around it at thirty paces and put two people on the headland
with poles to watch, and cut the field in sections, and if it comes at anybody, back off,
because it walks at thirty-four and a field-hand walks at fifty and there is nothing else
to know. She said the Hold had lived beside it for sixteen days already and had lost
nothing to it, and that she had walked the headland twice a day in that time, which is the
rota a Healer keeps for the living, and that she had kept it for her sister because nobody
had told her to stop.

Then she gave the fact that carried the room. Sable Kettle had put her thumb in the ground
at four paces from it and at eleven paces from it and at the fence, three times over the
sixteen days, and the ground was the same ground. No pale at the root. No seam of grey.
Nothing that had happened to the eleven paces around Rin Ashfall in four days had happened
to the west field in sixteen.

And Wren Ashfall said the thing that the Healers have said in this valley ever since,
which is that the Hold has never once been made safer by being made smaller.

She was asked about the Flask before she sat down. She had settled the ration at three by
the nineteenth day and Ketil Brack asked her for a fourth for anybody going into the west
field, and she refused it, and her reason was that she did not know what the ceiling was
and would not find it out on a wielder. She found it out anyway, four times, and their
names are in the thirty-fourth chapter with the dates, and they are not going in this one.

·  ·  ·

Colm Grey answered her and he was not answered back for twenty years.

He was one of the Hold's two Wardens. He had asked for the job on the third day and got it,
and he had walked out to the last fence twice on his own without telling anybody before
there was any reason to think that mattered, and on the eighth day he had stood on the
headland for eight hours watching Oda Ashfall cut nothing and had come in and said one
thing, and the thing he said was that she was not tired.

He said the following, and I have it in three accounts that agree closely enough that I
have run them together and marked nothing, because to mark it would be to pretend I had a
better one.

He said hold is not a haunting, it is a substance, and substances move. He said everybody
in the room had felt it move on the ninth day, out of a hole in the Memorial ground and
into twenty-two pairs of arms, and that nobody had found that strange because it had gone
the way they wanted. He said it goes the other way too, and it goes through ground, and the
proof of that is eleven paces of pale grass around a dead girl in Wrenfield and a stone
standing in the middle of it now to hold the pale off, and a Hold that has to raise a stone
over every field it wants to keep is a Hold that will run out of stone before it runs out
of fields.

Then he gave the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is why the Cull exists and why I will not
have it written up as cruelty.

A thing put down on its first day costs one swing. The same thing put down on its ninth
costs a body, and the body is a wielder, and a wielder costs a Succession, and a Succession
costs the Hold two names out of the labour pool for a day and a half and the third name
forever. He said the Hold had already paid that bill once. He said it had paid it because
nobody went out to Oda Ashfall on the seventh day, when she was a corpse in a field with
no grip on her, when Ketil Brack could have gone down with a stake and been home for
supper. He said the price of the four days the Hold spent being careful on the seventh and
the eighth and the ninth was Rin Ashfall on the fourteenth, and I have checked that claim
against every entry on the board and I cannot make it false and I cannot make it true.

And then he said the part that makes people put the book down, and I am copying it in full
because he said it in full, in a room with the dead woman's sister in it, and he did not
soften it and nobody has ever recorded that he raised his voice.

He said: anything carrying it, and anything that might be carrying it, goes down early and
goes down cheap. He said that includes the sick. He said he would rather say it out loud
in this room on the twenty-fourth day than have it done quietly by somebody in ten years
who had never had to argue for it. He said the holds that thought it and did not say it are
the ones with the tidy ledgers and the barred gates.

Nobody in the Hold answered that. Wren Ashfall said afterwards, to me, when she was old,
that she had not answered it because she had nothing to answer it with, and that she had
won the day on a rota and a thumb in the ground and knew it.

Colm Grey was right about the arithmetic. He is the reason this Hold was still standing
after the third Greying and he knew he was and said so, and on the hundred and fifty-second
day, when Wrenfield went to ash entire, it was the Cull's routes and the Cull's rule about
which ground you let go and when that got eleven people back inside a fence, and I will
write that chapter as it happened and it will not be comfortable either.

·  ·  ·

Sila Grey asked the question that neither of them could take.

She was a girl in the yard, working under Sable Kettle and learning the thumb, and she was
already known in the Hold for the thing she was known for the rest of her life, which was
that she would say the awkward half out loud in a room full of people who had agreed not
to. Everybody liked her. I want that on the page because of what she went on to do and
because of what the Hold went on to do about it.

She asked what the Burial had bought.

She said: if a woman is put in the ground whole with her gear and the Hold feels it and the
work goes better and it is cut on the board as a Keeping, and then sixteen days later there
is a thing in her field doing her stroke, then either the rite did not finish or the rite
was never what closed it, and if it was never what closed it then what the Hold has been
doing is saying her name over a hole.

Wren Ashfall said the rite finished and something else was standing.

Colm Grey said the rite finished and the field was not finished with.

Sila Grey said that those were two ways of saying nobody knows, and she was fifteen, and
she was not being clever, and she was correct.

Bran Holt did not write any of it down. I have it from three people, and one of them was
Sila Grey, sixty-one years old, with the same hands.

·  ·  ·

Maren Verren went down the headland at the middle of the afternoon with the Maul on her
shoulder and a hook in her right hand.

She had said nothing at the Hearth. She had said nothing at the Hearth on the eighth day
either, and she said nothing on the twenty-fourth, and in nine days of holding the blade
she had established the thing that the Hold came to rely on about her, which is that she
did not argue and then did the thing, and that you found out what she thought by watching
her back.

She went past the doorstone without touching it.

The Monument was standing in Wrenfield by then, five days old, eleven paces of grass
inside it green and loud and the pale stopped dead at the rim. She walked past that too,
on the way down, and Bel Brack, who was twelve and up on the byre wall where he had been
told not to be, said she did not look at it.

Then she went into the west field.

She walked a line that took her inside twenty-two of reach of the thing in it and she
walked it at an ordinary pace. It loaded the stroke. She had forty-five hundredths and she
used them walking. It finished the stroke high and left to right, in nothing, at the air
where a hook would have been, and by the time it had finished she was eight paces past it
with her back to it.

Then she bent down and cut barley.

She cut until the light went. Ketil Dunn came down at about the third hour with two others
and then four more, and by dark there were eleven people in the west field cutting barley
at between thirty and nine paces from Oda Ashfall's Husk, and two men on the headland with
poles who were not needed, and nobody spoke much, and the work got done.

That is the Healers' doctrine. It is not a speech. It is eleven people working in a field
with a dead woman in the middle of it, and the Hold did that for twenty years.

I asked four people what it was like. Three of them said it was the stroke. You would be
down over the barley and you would hear it start, the way you hear anybody start, and your
own body would answer it before you got hold of yourself. Ketil Dunn said that after the
first season you stopped hearing it. Wren Ashfall said that she never did and that she did
not want to.

Colm Grey was on the headland with a pole and he cut nothing and he stayed until the last
person came up, and what he said when he came in was that they could not do this every
season for twenty years. They did it for twenty years. He was still right.

·  ·  ·

The Hold paid for the twenty-fourth with a law, and the law was Colm Grey's and Wren
Ashfall asked for it herself.

His case was that if the Hold's position was that a Husk is owed a rite and not a blade,
then the Hold must never put the blade into the hand of anybody who held that position,
because a wielder who will not draw is a wielder who buries the people standing behind her.
He put it as a rota problem. He did not put it as a punishment and no account has him
putting it as a punishment.

Wren Ashfall agreed with him in front of everybody and then went further than he had asked.
She said a Healer should not be one of the three names called at a Succession, ever, under
any shortage, and that she was asking for it because she knew what she was and would not
have the Hold gamble on her at the worst hour it would ever have.

Bran Holt cut it into the board as a standing law on the twenty-fourth day.

It has never once been suspended. Not on the ninety-fifth, when the Hold was a Warden short
and put its own Teller in a kit. Not on the hundred and twenty-eighth. Not on the hundred
and forty-first, when there was a Healer in this Hold who had been outside the fence more
often than either of the two names that were called, and she was not called, and nobody in
the room thought it needed saying.

Wren Ashfall carried the blade ninth, on the hundred and ninety-seventh day. She was not a
Healer by then. How she stopped being one is the forty-second chapter's and not this one's.

·  ·  ·

Bran Holt also cut a second thing on the twenty-fourth, at Colm Grey's asking, and he cut
it on a stick and not on the board, because the board is for what the Hold did and the
stick was for what some of the Hold thought.

It was a roster. Colm Grey asked for a list of the names of the people who would go out
early and go out cheap and not have to be talked into it on the night, so that nobody would
ever have to hold a meeting about it again. Bran Holt cut a Warden's stick for it with a
cross for each name and he handed it to Colm Grey and wrote in the margin of the Chronicle
that he had done so and had not been asked to.

There was one cross on it on the twenty-fourth day.

There were nineteen on the forty-first, when it was read out at the Hearth over nine
charcoal burners with grey in the seams of their hands, and the reading of it is the
twenty-first chapter and I am not walking into it here.

Sable Kettle would not put her name on it. She was asked twice, on the twenty-fourth and
again years later, and she gave no reason either time, and her people were under the water
at Low Kettle with nothing kept for any of them, and I think that is the reason and I am
not going to write it down as the reason.

·  ·  ·

The pool stood at twenty-two on the twenty-fourth day.

It had stood at eighty-seven on the nineteenth, which is the most this Hold ever carried
before the hundred and twenty-eighth day, and forty of it went into the stone over Rin
Ashfall on the evening of the nineteenth, and twenty-five went into the Telling on the
twenty-first, and what was left going into the west field was twenty-two. On twenty-two the
Hold works at eighty-nine parts in a hundred, which is as close to whole as this settlement
was ever going to get, and nobody in it noticed, because nobody notices the absence of
standing water.

That is the arithmetic of the twenty-fourth day. The Hold was the lightest it ever was, the
crop came in, nothing was spent, and a woman walked past her mother-in-law inside the reach
of her arm and cut barley until dark.

Six of Mercy. Two of Communion. Three off Dominion.

Bran Holt cut the entry with the verb he had, and the verb he had has the word for an enemy
in it, and there are eighteen verbs on that board and there have only ever been eighteen. A
Warden whose name I do not have said, that evening, that the board wanted another word.
Bran Holt said there was no procedure for another word. He said it in the voice of a man
who had been over it already and had come out where he came out, and then he wrote down
that he had been asked, which is the most I have ever been able to get out of him about
anything.

Wren Ashfall sat at the table afterwards with her hands round a cup. It was not the same
cup. I know that because I asked her, when she was old and I was young and it was still the
kind of question I did not know better than to ask, and she looked at me for a while and
then said no, it was not, and then she told me to go and cut glass.
