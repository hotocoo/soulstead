# Chapter 25: The Quiet Doctrine

> *Held a Telling. Eleven names, one evening, in the order they were cut. The
> Forager held the graver.*
> Recorded: the board, in Bran Holt's hand, the sixty-first day

**Hand:** Bran Holt, second hand, then Grieve Mourne compiling
**Days:** 59 to 61
**Place:** the Hold; the Memorial

The law said hanging.

I put that first because Bran Holt put it first, and because every hand since has been
tempted to put it third. The Hold made a law against shaving a name off a pane on the
twenty-third day, which was one day after the first smear was found, which was before
anybody in the Hold had sat down and heard the argument for doing it. The law came first
and the reasoning came afterwards. The reasoning was thin. The law was not.

Thirty-six days later the Hold had the woman.

It did not hang her. It did not put her out of the gate, it did not take her off the
Forager rota, and it did not stop asking her which of two greys was the one that grows
barley, because there were three people in the Hold who could tell you that and she was
the best of the three.

What the Hold did was make her cut the names back in herself, eleven of them, in front of
everyone, and then hold a Telling for all eleven in one evening at a cost of twenty-five,
which the pool could just afford, and which left the Hold with six.

Bran Holt's entry for the sixty-first day is nine words long. I have read it more times
than I have read any other line in this book, and I still do not know whether it is the
best thing the Hold ever did or the most expensive piece of sentiment in the Chronicle,
and that is the honest position, and I am going to arrive at it slowly rather than start
there.

·  ·  ·

The argument was held at the Hearth on the fifty-ninth day, in the evening, with
twenty-six working people in the Hold and nineteen of them in the room.

Colm Grey wanted the hanging.

I want his case put properly, because it was a good case and because the man went out on
the hundred and ninth day and did not come back, and a Chronicle that makes its dead
sound stupid is not a Chronicle. He said the Hold had written a law and that a law nobody
performs is not a law, it is a wish with a date on it. He said the Hold had twenty-six
people and a palisade that was half timber and a Warden rota that could not cover the
mere road, and that a settlement that small cannot afford two sets of rules, one for the
thing it wrote down and one for the people it likes. He said that if the panes could be
shaved by somebody the Hold liked, they could be shaved by anybody, and that the next
person to do it would not stand up in the Hearth and say so.

Then he said the last part, which is the part Bran Holt wrote down verbatim, and it is
the only verbatim line in his entry for the fifty-ninth day.

*She took my brother's name off glass and I am the one being asked to be reasonable.*

Orrin Grey was his brother and hers. He had been dead since before Bran Holt started
keeping days, and he was in the near rows, and his was one of the eleven.

·  ·  ·

Sila Grey did not deny anything and did not ask for anything.

She was a Forager. She had been the girl in the yard on the sixteenth day, the one
standing at the fence with her hands full of two kinds of grey while an eleven-year-old
named a hammer, and she was thirty on the fifty-ninth, and she had the hands by then. She
stood up when Bran Holt asked her to and she put the whole theory out again, and every
hand that has copied this page has said the same thing about it, which is that she made it
better in the Hearth than she had made it in the dark.

The dead did not walk before we started writing them down. That is the first half and
nobody in the Vale has ever been able to answer it, including me, including in this
chapter.

The second half is what she added on the fifty-ninth day, and it was new.

She said she had not chosen the eleven by age. She said Verren Hold, two valleys east,
was shaving in order of age, oldest first, on the theory that the oldest names have been
calling the longest, and that she had heard the reasoning from a Wayfarer and had thought
about it for a season and did not believe it. She said a name is not calling because it is
old. A name is calling because it is being said.

So she had counted.

She had stood at the end of the Memorial range on ordinary afternoons, four times across
two seasons, and counted how many times each name on the glass was spoken out loud in a
day by people who were doing something else. Oda Ashfall came out highest, because of the
doorstone, because you cannot stand on a warm stone in a cold yard without somebody
saying whose it was. Rin Ashfall next. Sila Carver next, because the book gets opened.
Then the eight others, in an order she gave and Bran Holt wrote down and which I have
reproduced in the appendix because it is the only piece of measurement in this Chronicle
that nobody asked for.

The eleven were the eleven that get said. That is the whole of her selection, and she
would not pretend it was anything cleverer.

Ketil Brack asked her whether she had counted her own brother.

She said she had counted him and that he came fourth, and that she had done him first.

·  ·  ·

Now the part that made this a matter for the Hearth rather than a matter for the Wardens.

Four of the eleven are in ground the Hold can stand on and look at. The other seven are
ash, or are under crop in the near rows in places the Hold no longer marks, or are at
Wrenfield under stone. Of the four, two were quiet. Two were not.

Quiet means grass that behaves like grass. Not quiet means pale at root, which is the
first of the five states and the only one that ever arrives without anybody going
anywhere to fetch it, and it had been coming up over two of those four graves since about
the fortieth day.

And those graves are inside the fence line.

I have to be plain about why that emptied the Hearth of every argument except this one.
Work in the near rows is inside. The last fence is where outside starts. A Hold can go
eleven days without a body past the fence and nothing in the Vale moves one hair in that
time, and that is the oldest thing anybody here knows, and it is the thing the Hold steers
by. Pale at root inside the fence line is not supposed to be available. It arrives on
ground somebody has walked out onto, and the ground in question was ground the Hold
walked onto twice a day with a bucket.

So there was drawn-ash coming up under the bean rows in a Hold of twenty-six people, and
somebody had taken eleven names off eleven panes, and the two facts had a date between
them of about a fortnight, and nobody in the Hold could tell you which order they went in.

Sila Grey's reading: the pale is what opening looks like. A grip that is coming loose lets
go of the ground it has been holding, and grey comes up, and it stops when it has finished.

Bran Holt's reading, which he wrote in the same paragraph and did not privilege: the pale
is what a shaved man looks like when nobody will say his name.

Both are in. Neither explains the other. That is the fifty-ninth day.

·  ·  ·

Maren Verren ruled on the sixtieth.

She had been carrying the Maul for forty-five days by then and she had about twenty-four
left, and she said four things, and Bran Holt numbered them, which he did on three
occasions in seventy-six days and never explained.

One, no hanging. She gave no reason. Two hands have since supplied her a reason and I have
struck both out of the copy and left the absence where she left it.

Two, the names go back on. Not new panes. The same eleven panes, recut, in the same glass,
because a pane is pearl and the Hold did not have pearl to spare. The backlog stood at
nine. Nine of the Hold's own dead were in wax, waiting, and one of them was a child who had
been carried up out of a village that is under a lake, and she waited fourteen more days.

Three, the Forager cuts them. Herself. All eleven. In daylight.

Four, a Telling. One rite, eleven names, held the evening the last pane was finished.

The Carvers objected to the third one, and they were right to. Cutting glass is a trade.
The Hold had four Carvers on the sixtieth day, which is the most it ever had, and they
took the view that a Forager with a graver would ruin eleven panes the Hold could not
replace, and that a punishment which destroys the thing it is meant to restore is not a
punishment, it is a second offence with witnesses.

Asha Vane settled it, and she was the youngest of the four, and it was thirty-five days
before anybody pulled her out of that shed and put her in a Warden's kit and made her the
third hand of this book.

She ground the graver herself, to her own edge, and she sat at the Forager's right
shoulder on both evenings, and she did not touch the glass once.

She told me about it years afterwards, in the fast angry way she had by then, and what she
said was that she had spent the first evening waiting to take the tool off her and the
second evening watching her get better at it.

·  ·  ·

The recutting took two evenings, the sixtieth and the sixty-first, and the Memorial stood
open to the yard the whole time, which is the part that mattered and the part the Hold
argued about least.

A shaved pane does not look shaved. That is the first thing anyone learns about this. A
name cut into fluxed glass and then abraded off leaves a smear you can only see from one
angle, and the angle is low and to the left, and if you walk the range the way people walk
the range you see eleven names. Sila Grey had not hidden anything and had not needed to.
The Hold had been reading eleven names off eleven panes that did not have names on them
for about a fortnight, out of habit, off memory, the way you read a board you already know.

She said that out loud on the second evening while she was cutting, and it is the reason
this chapter exists.

She said she had got it wrong.

She said the glass is not where the calling happens. The glass is out here on the Memorial
ground, at the end of the yard, and a person comes to the Memorial twice in a season and
stands in front of it on purpose. The board is on the north wall of the Hearth at the
height of a grown person's eye, in the room where everybody eats, on the way through to
the byre, and it is read out loud, entire, by the Teller, at the turn of every season, in
front of the whole roll.

She said: I shaved the part you look at. I should have scraped the part you say.

And then she stopped, because she had not finished the thought and she knew it, and Bran
Holt wrote down the half of it that had words on it and left the rest. I have counted her
sentences on that page. There are five. The fifth one is the one I have built a life on
and she did not finish it.

What she was reaching for, and did not have for another twenty-odd years, is that there is
a difference between a name that is kept and a name that is called, and that she had never
once in her life asked anybody to forget anybody.

She said that part clearly and it is in the entry. *I am not asking you to lose him. I am
asking you to stop shouting at him.*

Nobody in the Hearth understood it. I did not understand it. I was nine.

·  ·  ·

On the sixty-first day, in the afternoon, while she was cutting the fourth of the eleven,
the pale over Orrin Grey stopped.

That is a flat statement and I am going to leave it flat. Two Field-hands had been marking
the edge of it with pegs since the forty-fourth day, because that is what this Hold does
with anything grey, and it had moved a hand's width every four days for four days at a
stretch and then sat still for eight and then moved again, and after the sixty-first day it
never moved again, and the pegs are still there, and the grass inside them is still pale
and is not spreading, and the beans have been cropped off that row nineteen times since.

The other grave that was not quiet stayed not quiet and went on slowly for another eleven
days and then stopped too, and nobody was cutting anything on the day it stopped.

So there are two facts and they are both in the Chronicle in the same hand on the same
leaf, and there are two explanations, and each explanation accounts for one of the facts
and makes the other one an accident.

A Teller will tell you the name went back on the glass and the ground settled.

Sila Grey told me, on the road back from the Memorial that evening, with the graver still
in her hand, that a shaved name takes as long to come open as the man took to close, and
that hers had come open on the sixty-first day after sixty days of quiet work, and that
she had been holding the tool that put it back when it did, and that she had thought about
whether to say so and had decided to say so.

I record what was said. I do not settle it. Four graves is not a number anybody can learn
anything from and both of these women knew that and neither of them stopped.

·  ·  ·

The Telling was held that evening.

A Telling is the death recounted aloud, in full, in front of everyone, by somebody who saw
it. That is the rite. It costs twenty-five and it gives every successor in this lineage,
forever, a purse that fills back faster by eight in the hundred when they stop swinging,
and I have carried that Legacy myself and I know exactly what it is worth because I stood
still on the two hundred and tenth day and counted while something enormous decided what
to do.

Eleven names in one rite had never been done and has not been done since.

Four of the eleven had nobody left who had seen it. Ketil Dunn went before Bran Holt
started counting and there is no entry for him at all. Two of the four had been in the
ground longer than the Hold had been keeping panes, which means their panes were cut from
memory in the first place, by Sila Carver, who was herself the third name on the list.

What the Hold did about the four was say what it knew and then say that it did not know
the rest, out loud, in front of everybody, and Bran Holt entered the gaps as gaps.

The board paid the full eight of Memory anyway. I have looked at that for twenty years.
The board does not audit. It counts whether a thing was held.

The order, which is the order the panes were cut in the first place and not the order Sila
Grey counted them in:

Oda Ashfall. Rin Ashfall. Sila Carver. Bel Brack. Ketil Dunn. Orrin Grey. Asha Kettle.
Colm Stonewake. Wren Ember. Tarn Holt. Sable Vane.

Seven of those eleven given names belong also to people who later took up the blade in
this Hold or kept this book. That is not significance. It is a valley with eighteen names
in it, and I have written about that elsewhere and been accused of making too much of it
by a woman who had four of her own on a wall.

Two of the eleven were a wielder's kin on the sixty-first day, and both of them are
Ashfalls, and Rin Ashfall was a wielder and also a wielder's wife, which is the only time
in the lineage that has happened. By the hundred and ninth day two more of the eleven were
a wielder's kin and neither of them had moved.

Maren Verren told Rin Ashfall's death, which had already been told once, on the
twenty-first day, by Maren Verren, and told wrong in two particulars.

On the twenty-first day she said three Cinder Stalkers and she said grass.

On the sixty-first day she said four and she said loose ash at the foot of the cut bank.

Bran Holt wrote both versions on the same leaf, one under the other, and drew no line
between them and made no note. It is the cleanest thing the second hand ever did and I do
not think he knew he was doing it.

And there is a further trouble with that Telling which I am obliged to put down, because
the rite requires somebody who saw it. The first hand's account of the nineteenth day has
Maren Verren going in on the downhill side because the loose ash was on that side and she
had watched her wife die on it. The second hand's account of the fourteenth day has an
eleven-year-old boy on the palisade frame, where he was not supposed to be, as the only
person who saw anything at all, and the boy was four days dead by the time this was told
and cannot be asked.

Both hands are in this book. Both are copied out three times and carried east. I have held
the leaves up to the fire and neither has been scraped.

I record what was said.

·  ·  ·

I was in the Memorial that evening. I was nine. I held the water bowl that the graver gets
cooled in, because somebody put it in my hands and told me not to spill it, and I did not
spill it, and I am in this chapter as far as that sentence goes and no further.

On the way back across the yard Sila Grey told me that Maren Verren had never once touched
the doorstone. Not going out, not coming in, not in sixty-nine days. Everyone else in the
Hold touched it flat with two fingers the way the first wielder had, and the first
wielder's wife walked round it.

She told me the same thing again, in the same words, about twenty years later, and I was
too old by then to ask her why either time.

·  ·  ·

Here is the honest position and then I will stop.

The Unnaming may be right.

There is no instrument in this Vale that can tell you. Sixty days of shaving produced one
grave that stopped and one that did not, and the one that stopped was being recut at the
time. That is not evidence of anything. It is two coincidences arguing.

And if they are right, then the thing I am doing now is the thing that has been killing
this valley. Every name I cut into glass is a hand held closed. Every pane in that range
is a grip somebody could have walked away from. Every Telling this Hold has ever held,
every one that put eight on the board and eight in the hundred into the arm of every
person who came after, was paid for with a name kept loud, and the fields north of here
are ash, and the low rows are going pale, and I have four hundred pages of it in my own
hand on a shelf I built.

I have thought about that for twenty years and I have kept writing, and I want it recorded
that I kept writing without having answered it, because the alternative was to stop
writing on a suspicion, and a hold that stops writing on a suspicion cannot afterwards
tell you what it suspected.

The Hold did not hang her. It made her cut eleven names into glass with her own hands in
daylight in front of everybody, which is either the mildest sentence in the Chronicle or
the cruellest, depending on whether she was right.

She cut them well. Asha Vane said so and Asha Vane was not generous.

Years after this chapter a copy came west in which a hold of a hundred and fifteen people
had taken this road to the end of itself, and I gave it to her to read because she was the
only person in the Vale entitled to it, and she kept it a season and brought it back with
one judgement and no argument attached.

She said they did it in the wrong order.

## Other Roads

### Joining them

Verren Hold, two valleys east, a hundred and fifteen working people, which is four and a
half of us. Panes shaved in order of age, oldest first, on the theory that the oldest
names have been calling the longest.

They did not do it in the dark. It was put to the roll on their thirty-fourth day at the
end of a long evening and it carried seventy-one to forty-four, and the forty-four stayed,
and were on the rota the next morning. It was not a cult and it was not a coup. It was a
testable proposition, and a settlement tested it.

It worked for sixty days. On their forty-sixth a ring in the north rows opened by itself
with nobody inside two hundred paces of it, and the whole roll walked out to stand and look
at grass. Nothing in this Chronicle has ever done that. Their pool reached nothing on their
sixty-first with no Wake held for it, their output ran full for two seasons, and for a
stretch Verren Hold was the hold the Wayfarers walked to.

Their second hand found the bill on their ninety-sixth. Six Legacies, all of them from
before the vote. A Legacy comes from a rite, a rite costs grief, and grief comes only from
a death entered with a name and a count of bonds. Eight wielders in fifty-one days after
that, their hundred and thirty-ninth to their hundred and ninetieth, every one left where
they fell. Their hundred and ninety-first entry is one line: `Mem -41`.

Their last thirty-seven entries are signed *Kept by N. Verren*. Nix Verren fell on their
two hundred and third, and the entry recording his death is in the same hand as the
signatures.

There is no verb for shaving a pane. The board sees only the absence of what the act
prevents, and then the abandonments.

### Ignoring it

The Hold finds eleven smears, cannot prove who, and lets the season turn.

Four holds in my copies did exactly this and there is nothing in any of the four boards to
mark it, because nothing happened that has a verb. No hanging, no ruling, no recutting, no
rite.

What it costs is the rite. No Telling is held on the sixty-first day, so eight of Memory,
four of Mercy and five of Communion are not entered, and the eight in the hundred that
comes back to every successor in this lineage when they stop swinging does not exist, and
on the two hundred and tenth day a man of thirty-two with a graver's forearms stands in
front of something at the ninth ring and does not have it.

### Hanging her

There is a valley where this was done, and their Teller had to choose a verb, and there
are only eighteen, and he chose `enemy_slain`.

I have copied that line out four times in my life and I have never once managed to copy it
without stopping. *Put down the Forager.* The board took nothing off Memory for it, because
the board has no opinion about who a name belongs to, and it put two on Dominion, because
the board does understand a settlement asserting itself.

What the copy records after it is one season of a hold with no argument in it. Their
Foragers stopped bringing back the greys they were not certain about. Their third hand
stopped entering the Readings aloud at the turn, because the fifth line had become a thing
people looked at each other about. Two of their four Healers went east.

**Pressure:** toward **The Long Hold**, and it is worth being exact about how, because it
is not the two on Dominion that does it. It is that a hold which has hanged its own best
Forager for an argument cannot afterwards hear one, and a hold that cannot hear an argument
opens its gate to nobody, and shuts it eventually against everybody, and the last thing it
does is bar it from the inside.
