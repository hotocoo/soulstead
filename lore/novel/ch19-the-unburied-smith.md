# Chapter 19: The Unburied Smith

> *Bel Brack. Seventeen. Six years at the fire. Twenty-eight.*
> Recorded: the pane in the south range, cut in the fourth hand out of the boy's own
> pearl, which the Hold kept in a covered jar for eleven years waiting for somebody who
> could cut glass

**Hand:** Bran Holt, second hand
**Days:** 29 to 31
**Place:** the Smithy; the Memorial ground

This is the chapter in which Ashfall Hold burned somebody for the first time, and it sits
in the side roads because what the Hold decided on the thirty-first day was not a death.
It was a manufacture. Every cake of soap this valley has ever sold anybody, and every
pane of glass in the west range and the south range with a name cut into it, and every
flask any wielder after the thirty-first day ever drank in a field, comes off four
flagstones that were laid in one afternoon because until that afternoon nobody had
thought of laying them.

Bel Brack was eleven when he named the Widow's Maul in the yard. He was seventeen when he
came down into the hot end.

On the board that is fifteen days. In the Hold's own talk it was six years. Both of those
are in this Chronicle and both of them are correct, because they are two instruments and
they were never the same instrument. Bran Holt wrote days. Ketil Brack counted summers,
and when I put the question to him, late, he answered me in summers and did not notice he
had. I have left both as they were written.

·  ·  ·

What happened in the six years is the ordinary thing.

He got tall. He got strong in the one arm first, the way apprentices do, and then in the
other, and by his fourth summer he had stopped being the boy who was up on things and
become the one who was up on the roof because somebody had sent him. He was good. I want
that on the page before anything else, because the Hold's account of him afterwards was
sentimental about it and he did not need the help.

By the fifth summer he was doing the Smithy's small iron alone. The cramps that hold the
timber on the north-east arc of the palisade were drawn by him. They were still holding on
the hundred and thirty-fourth day when something came over that timber at head height,
and the timber held, and the cramps held, and I have been up on the walk and checked them
with my own hand, which is not a thing a Teller is for, and I did it anyway.

And he seated the wedge in the Widow's Maul.

The Maul is set on a wedge instead of a pin so that a wielder can reset the head in a
field, which was Ketil Brack's design, and it was the first piece of design made in this
Vale by a man who expected somebody to be out for days. What the design means in the
working of it is that somebody has to seat the wedge, and seating a wedge badly is worse
than a pin, and from the boy's fourth summer Ketil Brack stopped doing it himself.

Maren Verren carried the Maul into the Smithy and put it on the bench and the boy did it
and she waited. They did not talk much. This went on for three years.

The wedge has been reset nine times if you count the seatings. Four of the nine are his.

·  ·  ·

The hot end was his work too, and the hot end is what killed him.

The Smithy has two fires. The one at the near wall is for iron and it is the fire the Hold
had from the first year. The other is at the gable and it is small and it is built up in
courses of rock off the scarp, and what it is for is glass, and glass will not run at the
heat a wood fire reaches. The Carvers had asked for it. There were names in the Hold that
had no pane, and the Memorial is not a wall of stone, it is a wall of glass, and glass
wants a hood over the fire drawing hard enough to make the difference.

Nobody in the Hold had built a hood. Bel Brack was building one.

He was on the frame over the gable on the twenty-ninth day with a bar in both hands. Both
hands is the whole of it. A man with one hand free catches something on the way down and a
man with a bar in both hands arrives.

Ketil Brack got him out. He got him out of the hot end with his own hands and his own
hands were six years at that fire and knew exactly what they were doing, and they did it,
and he was a month mending afterwards and worked through it. The Healers did what there is
to do. On the twenty-ninth day of the board, in the fourth summer of the hot end, that was
not much.

The boy lived the rest of that day and the night. He died in the morning of the
thirty-first and he died at the fire, on the bench along the near wall, because Ketil
Brack would not have him carried anywhere and nobody put it to him twice.

I have Bran Holt's entry and I have four testimonies and they agree. There is nothing in
any of them that anybody said. He was seventeen and he had been six years learning a trade
and the last thing he did was get up on a frame he had been getting up on all week.

·  ·  ·

Ketil Brack's position was given once, on the evening of the twenty-ninth, in the Smithy,
with the boy still breathing on the bench, and it did not change in two days.

He said the gear was the boy's grip. He said it in those words. He said six years of it
was in the hammer and the apron and the tongs and burning them was burning him, and that
the ground was where a grip goes, whole, with the things it closed on, which was what Sila
Carver had reasoned on the ninth day and it had worked and nobody had improved on it
since.

He was not wrong about any part of that. I will put the Hold's need next to it and not on
top of it.

The Hold wanted a Pyre.

Not for piety. The kilns are under the barrows. Lime comes off Stonewake and burnt shell
comes off the mere and ground bone comes off both, and all three of them go back into the
furrows, because ash ground locks and the only thing anybody in this Vale has ever found
that unlocks it is what a Hold puts back. A season of near rows is a season of somebody
walking under the barrows with a basket.

Maren Verren had walked under them on the twenty-seventh and one of the upright things had
come down off the shoulder of the quarry at her, and she had killed it, and she had come
in at dusk with three flasks and all three of them empty.

Three was the ration. The Healers had settled three by the nineteenth day and the settling
of it is a chapter of its own and a bad one, and it was not caution.

A Pyre raises the ration by one. It raises it for every wielder who ever carries anything
afterwards, and it cannot be lost, and that is not a thing anybody in the Vale has ever
found a way to argue with.

·  ·  ·

Bran Holt wrote it out on the board on the evening of the twenty-ninth in the plain way,
because writing things out in the plain way was the whole of what he was for.

```
Pool stands at fifty.
Burial takes twenty and leaves thirty.
Pyre takes thirty and leaves twenty.
A Burial gives six on the body of every successor after and cannot be lost.
A Pyre gives one flask on the ration of every successor after and cannot be lost.
Wielder went to the scarp on the twenty-seventh with three and came in with three empty.
Lime wanted at the kilns before the turn. The kilns are under the barrows.
Shell wanted off the mere. Nobody has been down to the mere.
```

Fifty is the pool because of the arithmetic and the arithmetic is ten to begin with and
three for every bond, and nobody has ever explained why it is ten, and the pool had been
sitting at twenty-two since the Telling on the twenty-first.

Bel Brack had six bonds. Ketil Brack, six years. Maren Verren, three years of a wedge.
Bran Holt himself, who had the boy on the tally on the days the hip was bad and wrote his
own name into the count without any visible difficulty. Rin Ashfall, six years dead, whom
he had followed about when he was eleven and whose dying he was the only person in this
Vale to see. Sila Carver, dead on the twenty-eighth, three days and one winter earlier,
who had let him hold the graver and had not let anybody else. And Yeva Holt, who was
fifteen and worked the bellows on the days he was up on the roof.

Ten and eighteen. Twenty-eight. Twenty-two and twenty-eight is fifty.

Fifty on a Hold of twenty-two working people puts the work at three parts in four. It is
not ruinous. Bran wrote the fraction beside the figure because he wrote every fraction
beside every figure for seventy-six days and the Hold has never sufficiently thanked him
for it.

He did not write a fifth line in the list, and every hand since has noticed the absence of
it.

The fifth line would have said that Oda Ashfall stopped on the sixth day and stood up on
the eighth, and that a Smithy is not a field, it is a building in the middle of a
settlement with a door onto the yard, and that in six years nobody in this Vale had found
out whether a smith's apprentice comes up with a hammer in his hand or with the bellows
in it, and nobody has found out since, because in this one case they did not let it
happen.

I have asked the two people who were in that Hold and were still alive when I came to ask,
whether he left the line off out of decency or out of the discipline. I got two answers. I
record what was said, and I do not settle it, and I will add only that Bran Holt was a man
who recorded exactly what he was asked to record and nothing else, and that this is the one
page in the second hand where that habit looks like mercy.

·  ·  ·

Maren Verren did not argue with Ketil Brack. She is recorded as having said four things in
two days and none of them was about the boy.

She worked the fire.

She came in at first light on the thirtieth with her sleeves already up and she took the
bellows, and she was a Field-hand, Steady and Scarred, twenty-six years old when she took
the blade and older than that by then, and she had never worked a fire in her life. She
did the small iron. Cramps for the arc, and nails, and a hook, and she did all of it badly
and she did it all day. Ketil Brack sat on the bench by the boy and said nothing and
corrected her twice.

On the second morning she was back on the bellows before he was in the room.

Here is what the Hold saw, and I have this from four people and they do not differ. They
saw the wielder of Ashfall Hold, the only person in the settlement who could go past the
fence and come back, spending two of her days pumping a bellows badly, in the room, with
the boy on the bench, while the board outside in the Hearth said fifty and said twenty and
said thirty and said three empty flasks. Nobody had to say a word of it out loud. Every
single person in that Hold could do the sum, because Bran Holt had spent nine days
teaching them to, and he had taught them on purpose, and he wrote afterwards that he was
not sure he should have.

She let the arithmetic say itself. That is the whole of what she did and it took her two
days and it worked and it has been held against her in this valley ever since by people
who would have argued instead and got a worse answer slower.

A Warden of that time, who is on no roster I can name because Bran did not name him, is
given twice as having said afterwards that it was not gentleness. He was right. It was not
gentleness and she never once claimed it was, and the same two days that got the Hold its
fourth flask got a man off the cart ground on the hundred and thirty-fourth day, and she
was fifty days dead by then and had no say in it either way.

·  ·  ·

On the evening of the thirty-first Ketil Brack got up off the bench and went to the end of
it where the gear was laid out, because the Healers lay gear out, and he carried it to the
Memorial ground himself.

The hammer. The apron, which was cut down out of one of his own. The tongs the boy had
made in his second summer, badly, and kept, and used, and never replaced. Two files. A
knife. The boots.

He said one thing on the way, to nobody, and two people heard it and give it in the same
words. He said the boy would not let him make him another hammer.

That is all of it. I have three fuller versions of that walk across the yard, one of them
in verse, from a Wayfarer copy made four valleys west by somebody who was not born yet,
and I have not used any of them.

·  ·  ·

There was no place to do it.

This is the part of the thirty-first day that the Vale forgets, and it is the part with
consequences. A Burial needs a hole. A Pyre needs a floor, because a fire on open ground
goes into the ground, and what a Pyre is for is the ash, and ash in soil is ash you have
given away to a field you were not ready to give it to.

Nobody had worked that out in advance. Four Carvers worked it out in an afternoon.

They laid four flags off the scarp on the Memorial ground, level, with a lip, and they
sanded the joints, and they did it in about five hours with two of them arguing the whole
time about the fall of the surface, and one of those two was Asha Vane and she won, and
the flags are there now, and the lip is worn down on the east side where two hundred days
of rakes have come across it.

Everything comes off those flags. I am going to state it once and let it sit.

The body and the gear burn together. What is left is raked up off the stone while it is
still warm and put into water and steeped. It stands three days and settles. It is drawn
off clear, and what is drawn off clear is lye, and lye is soap, and soap is the only thing
Ashfall Hold has ever sold anybody, and lye boiled dry in a pot is pot-ash, and pot-ash
refined again is the fine white powder the Carvers call pearl, and pearl put into sand at
the hot end lets glass run at a heat a wood fire cannot reach. The Memorial's panes are
glass. Every pane is fluxed with pearl drawn from the pyre of the person whose name is cut
into it, so that the name is written in the substance of the named. And the rest of the
lye and the ground bone go back into the furrows, because the Hold feeds its fields its
dead, and that is not a figure of speech, it is how you get barley in the fifth season on
ash ground.

And some of what is drawn off clear is not made into soap and not put on a field. It is
cut with hearth-water until it will not strip a throat, and it is put in a flask, and it is
drunk. It burns going down and it keeps burning. What it does is close a grip that has come
open: it gives a body back the hold it has already spent, briefly, and at a cost.

The Pyre on the thirty-first day raised the ration of Ashfall Hold from three to four.

The fourth charge in this Hold is a smith's apprentice who was seventeen. Tarn Stonewake
drank it in the cart ground on the hundred and thirty-fourth day and the Healers gave it
to him without being asked and wrote it down. I drank it on the two hundred and tenth.

·  ·  ·

They burned him at dusk and it took most of the night.

Bran Holt's entry for the thirty-first day is one line and the four numbers, and then the
sentence he was required to write, and then nothing. *Held a Pyre.* Plus eight, plus four,
plus five, nothing on the fourth. Thirty out of the pool and twenty left standing, and the
work came back to nine parts in ten inside a week.

Ketil Brack was at the Smithy fire the following morning. He had a hood to finish and
nobody else in the Hold could have finished it, and he finished it, and the hot end has
run every pane in this Memorial since, including the boy's, which is in the south range
and which I cut myself, out of his own pearl, eleven years later, and which I got right.

The Hold did not hold a Telling for him. Twenty-five was in the pool and they had it and
they did not spend it, and the reason given at the time, by two people, was that the only
person who could have told it properly was on the bench with him for two days and would
not be asked. I have never been able to decide whether that is true or whether the Hold
simply wanted its twenty-five, and Bran Holt wrote no note on it, and I record what was
said.

Maren Verren went past the fence on the thirty-third day carrying four flasks, which
nobody in this Vale had ever done, and she did not go to the scarp.

The water had dropped. That is the next chapter.

## The Other Roads

### The gear taken by force

There is no verb for it. That is the first thing to say about this road, and it is the
reason the road is hard to see from inside a Chronicle: the board has eighteen verbs and
it has had eighteen from the twentieth day and none of the eighteen is *took a man's tools
off him*.

I have it from Verren Hold, two valleys east, in a Wayfarer copy I trust as far as its
sixtieth page. A smith there would not give up an apprentice's gear and the hold wanted the
ash, and four of them went into the shop at night and broke him open and took it, and they
held their Pyre, and their Teller sat with the list of eighteen and chose the nearest
thing on it, and the nearest thing on the list is not the thing.

### The name shaved instead

A Forager came to Ketil Brack on the thirtieth and made the offer properly and without
defiance, and Bran Holt wrote down that the offer was made and did not write down who made
it, and I have a reasonable guess and I am not putting a guess in a Chronicle.

The offer was this. Do not burn him. Do not bury him. Cut no pane. Stop saying the name,
and take the name off nothing because there is nothing yet to take it off, which makes him
the easiest case the Unnaming had ever been handed. The dead of this Vale did not walk
before this Vale started writing them down. A grip stays closed because it is being
called. Let go of the calling and it opens on its own and nobody has to walk out to
anybody with a maul.

The board cannot record this. There is no verb for a shaving and there has never been one,
which the Unnaming say is the Tellers protecting the trade and which the Tellers say is
because you cannot enter a thing that leaves no mark, and both of those are arguments and
neither of them is a demonstration.

I have one copy in which a hold did it, to a girl of nine, and kept a board afterwards.
The girl stood up on the second day. The entry is there and the line the Teller wrote is
the one the board gives him, with nothing in the place where the name goes.
