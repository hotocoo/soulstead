# Chapter 12: The Reclaiming of Rin

> *Four days. Second growth. She went at noon because she would not go at dawn and she
> would not go at dusk, and noon was the only hour left that meant nothing.*
> Recorded: Sila Carver, first hand, last entry

**Hand:** Sila Carver, first hand, closing
**Days:** 19
**Place:** Wrenfield, past the last fence; then the Memorial ground

Four days is late. The Hold did not know it was late on the nineteenth. It knows now,
because every hand since has had the same table to read from and the table says two days
to a growth, and four days is two growths, and two growths is a different animal.

Maren Verren went out at noon with a post-driver and came back at the middle of the
afternoon with two weapons and a dead woman's hands to wash, and between those two facts
is the first full account of a reclaiming anybody in the Vale ever wrote down. Sila cut it
over two evenings and it is the longest single entry in the first hand and the last thing
she ever wrote.

·  ·  ·

She did not go at dawn. Bran Holt wanted dawn, because the crust holds at dawn and loose
ash under grass is what had put Rin Ashfall on one knee. Ketil Brack wanted dusk, on the
reasoning that a thing which has been standing in a field for four days has been standing
in the sun for four days and might be slower when the ground cooled, which was not
reasoning, which was hope with a tool in its hand.

She went at noon.

Sila's line about the hours is the only piece of the first hand that has ever been called
literature, and Sila would have been annoyed by that. What she was recording was a
practical objection. Dawn was the hour Rin went out. Dusk was the hour Rin came back.
Maren Verren was not going to walk into the long grass at either of them.

She took the Maul and three flasks and nothing else. The Healers had settled on three by
then, and the settling of it is a chapter of its own and a bad one. Three was what she
had.

Bel Brack was not on the palisade frame. Ketil had nailed a board across the ladder on the
sixteenth and had then stood at the foot of it for most of the nineteenth, which is the
only time in that whole month that anybody managed to keep the boy off anything.

So there is no witness. Everything in the next four pages comes from Maren Verren, told
once, that evening, at the table, to Sila Carver, who wrote it down while she was talking
and did not go back and tidy it. It is the only account of a fight in the Chronicle that
was taken down from a person still shaking, and it reads like it, and no hand since has
touched a word.

·  ·  ·

The ring in the grass was eleven paces across by then. It had been three on the
fourteenth.

The grass inside it was not dead. It was bent, all of it, inward, the whole ring leaning
toward the middle like a crowd at a table, and pale at the root all the way out to the
rim. Past the rim the grass stood up green. You could put your hand on the line between
them.

The thing in the middle was standing where she had gone down, in the same place, facing up
the slope at the cut bank.

Maren Verren said it had her wife's shoulders. She said that first, before anything else,
and Sila wrote it first, and I have left it first.

It had the Ashblade. It had the coulter's curve and the cart iron and the whole of Ketil
Brack's two days of work, and it had them in a grip that had closed four days ago and had
not opened since, and it had, by then, things Rin Ashfall never had. That is what a growth
is. Not a bigger animal. A better one. Two growths on a field like that one and what is
standing there has more in the purse than the woman ever carried, hits harder than she
could, and has learned a habit she did not have.

The habit it had learned was to step into recovery.

Rin Ashfall never did that. Rin Ashfall swung and then stood in the fifth of a second
afterwards like everybody does the first month. The thing in the ring had spent four days
in a field being fed, and what the field had taught it was to watch for the end of a
stroke and be inside it when it finished.

Maren Verren found that out on her first swing and it cost her the whole of her left
forearm's skin from the elbow down.

·  ·  ·

The spouse's arithmetic ran both ways inside the ring and it ran both ways at once.

She hit harder in there than she had ever hit. She said so. She said the head went through
the first exchange in a way it had not gone through anything in the yard, and she did not
know why and she did not stop to work it out, and it has been worked out since by people
with better instruments than a widow in a field: what is in the ground in a ring like that
is hers. It is not being given back and it is not being kept from her either. It is lying
between them, and the two of them are both reaching, and she is standing on it.

And she emptied faster in there than she had ever emptied. Four swings. She said four and
Sila wrote four and I have seen no reason to doubt it. Four swings of the Maul and she was
at the bottom of the purse, and she had done that in the yard nine times and the yard had
told her six.

Those are the same fact. I want to be plain about this because the Vale has spent two
hundred days being sentimental about it and it is not a sentiment, it is a mechanism. The
reason she could hit like that is the reason she ran out like that. The part of her that
was out in the field was doing both. A woman standing on her own grip can spend it faster
than she owns it, and the bill arrives in the same minute.

Every spouse since has been told this at the Succession, in about that many words, and
every spouse since has taken the blade anyway.

·  ·  ·

The fight took a long time. She did not know how long. Sila asked and Maren said she did
not know, and Sila wrote *she does not know*, which is a better entry than a number.

What we have is five heavy swings and one flask, in this order.

The **first** she has already given us: it went through, and the thing stepped into the end
of it and opened her forearm.

The **second** she threw away on purpose. She said she wanted to see the step again. She
committed to a swing she knew would not land, watched it come in on the end of her
recovery, and took a hit across the hip for the information. Bran Holt, when he heard
this, said it was the best piece of work anybody in the Hold had done since the Vale, and
he said it in a tone that made his wife leave the room.

The **third** landed on the packing.

There was no packing. That is the point. A Gravewarden has plate stuffed with borrowed
grip and you cannot get in until you have broken it, and the thing in the ring had
nothing on it but a dead woman's coat. And it did not matter, because it did not go down.
It took the Maul across the chest, twenty-four of it, at noon, and it staggered and it
came back, and Maren Verren discovered what a second growth actually means and had to
stand there and discover it with nothing in the purse.

She drank then. One flask. Pyre ash steeped and drawn off clear and cut with hearth-water,
and it burns going down and it keeps burning, and what it does is close a grip that has
come open. She said it was like being handed back something she had already spent and
being told to be quick about it.

Two left.

The **fourth** she did not swing. She backed up the slope into the standing grass, out of
the ring, and the thing came to the rim and stopped.

That is a fact and the Chronicle has it. It came to the rim and stopped. It did not follow
her out. On the nineteenth day nobody knew what that meant. It has been observed eleven
times since and it holds until the third growth, at which point it stops holding, and
that single change is the reason the Hold has ever had to defend a palisade.

She stood in the green grass and let the purse fill, which it does not do quickly and does
not do at all if you are still moving, and she was not still moving, because she was
bleeding down the inside of her arm and shaking, and she counted. She told Sila she
counted and she did not say what she counted to.

Then the **fifth**.

·  ·  ·

She went in on the downhill side because that was the side the loose ash was on and she
had watched her wife die on it, and her reasoning, which she gave to Sila in one sentence,
was that a thing standing in a ring for four days does not know the ground has changed
under it.

It swung at her. It swung with the Ashblade, going heavy, which is more than a quarter
second of wind-up and a hair under four tenths of being unable to do anything at all
afterwards.

She met it with the haft.

She did not mean to. This is the thing the Wardens have never been able to teach and the
thing they have never stopped trying to teach: that if you put something in the way at the
exact moment a stroke is arriving, and the moment is about twelve hundredths of a second
wide, then what comes back to you is not the blow. What comes back to you is the spending.
The thing that swung has paid for the swing and you have taken the payment.

Maren Verren put the ash haft up because her arm was going and she wanted something
between her and the blade, and the timing was luck, and she said so herself, at the table,
that evening: *I did not mean it. Write that I did not mean it.* Sila wrote it.

The Ashblade came off the haft. The thing that had been holding it went back a half pace
with its guard open and nothing in it, and it was standing on loose ash, and it went down
onto one knee.

Then the fifth heavy swing.

Sila's entry for what the fifth swing did is four words and I am not going to improve on
them either. *It went down properly.*

·  ·  ·

What happens at a reclaiming is this, and the Hold had it once, on the nineteenth, and has
had it ten times since, and it has never once been less strange.

The grip opens. Not slowly. The ring of bent grass stands up, all of it, in about the time
it takes to draw a breath, out to the rim, eleven paces, and the pale at the root stays
pale but stops spreading that hour and never resumes. And what was in the closed hand
comes back to whoever is standing there.

She came home with two weapons. The Maul, with the wedge loose, and the Ashblade, with
the coulter's curve, and Ketil Brack's two days of work, and four days of weather on it,
and it needed nothing done to it at all.

That is the whole mechanism and I have written it plainly because the Vale has an
unfortunate way of talking about it. What comes back is the loadout. Not the person.
Nobody has ever got a person back. What you get back is the iron and the trick of the wrist
that used it, and the Hold has been paying for that exchange in bodies for two hundred
days and would pay again tomorrow, and does.

The Hold had two weapons and one wielder on the evening of the nineteenth day, and the
Ashblade went on a hook on the Smithy wall where it stayed for ninety-one days until Yeva
Holt took it down.

·  ·  ·

And there was the other thing that came out of the ring, which nobody had expected, and
which changed the arithmetic of the whole Vale.

The grief came back.

When the grip opens it lets go of everything it has been holding, and what a four-day
Echo in a fed field has been holding is a great deal. Bran Holt was at the fence with the
board and he watched the numbers and he said afterwards, in his flat way, that the Hold had
been carrying forty-two and was carrying eighty-seven before Maren Verren reached the
gate.

This is the part of the Vale's economy that no outsider believes when a Wayfarer tells
them. An Echo is a purse too. It sits in a field storing what the ground draws and what
the Hold cannot spend, and when it goes down, all of it arrives at once, on a settlement
that is standing in a yard watching a woman walk in with two weapons.

Eighty-seven of grief on a Hold of twenty-two is work at something over half. It is bad.
It is also, and this is the whole hinge of the Vale, the only reason a Monument was
possible before the season turned. A rite is not paid for with goodwill. It is paid for
with grief, and grief is what a reclaiming hands you, and a Hold that leaves its dead out
in the fields is a Hold that cannot afford to bury anybody.

Every Teller works this out in their first year and every Teller writes it down as though
they are the first. Here is mine, and it is Bran Holt's really, from the twentieth: *the
ones who go and get them can pay for the funerals. The ones who do not, cannot.*

·  ·  ·

They raised the stone on the evening of the nineteenth, in the ring, which was standing
grass again by then.

Forty of grief. It is the most expensive rite the Hold had performed and the second most
expensive there is.

Ketil Brack brought the relic. It had been on a shelf in the Smithy for six days, since
the thirteenth, since the barrow at Stonewake, a dense warm knuckle of matter shaped like
nothing, slightly wrong in the hand, and he had spent those six days not knowing what to
do with it and had settled on nothing. Sila Carver told him to put it under the stone.

He asked why.

She said because a name on glass holds a name and a stone with one of those under it holds
ground, and she had no evidence for the second half of that and said so, and did it
anyway.

It worked. That is the flat fact and it is the beginning of the Carvers' whole trade. The
pale at the root inside eleven paces of that stone stopped that evening and has not
resumed in two hundred days. Wrenfield went to Gone in the hundred and fifty-second day,
all of it, everything from the last fence to the cut bank, and there is an eleven-pace
circle of Quick grass in the middle of it with a stone in the middle of that, and the
Foragers walk out to it in the ash and stand in it and come back and will not talk about
what it is like.

Maren Verren said nothing at the stone. Sila asked her, later, whether she wanted to say
anything, and she said the saying was a different rite and she was not ready for it, and
that is the earliest evidence anybody has that the Hold already understood a Telling to be
a separate thing from a Burial, two days before it held one.

·  ·  ·

Sila Carver cut three entries that night, which is the most anybody had cut in one evening,
and then she sat at the table with Maren Verren, and the cup was still there, and one of
them moved it. Neither ever said which.

Sila Carver died on the twenty-eighth day of nothing in particular, in her bed, at
fifty-four, and the second hand begins on the twentieth because Bran Holt had been doing
the numbers for a week by then and simply kept the graver when she handed it to him to
check a figure.

She has a pane in the west range. She cut the first two in the Vale, for Oda Ashfall and
for Rin Ashfall, and somebody else had to cut hers, and whoever did it got the S wrong and
did not start again.
