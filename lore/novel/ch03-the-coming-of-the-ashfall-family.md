# Chapter 3: The Coming of the Ashfall Family

> *D4. No weather. Nothng spent. K. Brck says the day was not took off us.*
>
> Sila Carver, first hand, dated the fourth day, cut on the ninth with the rest

**Hand:** Sila Carver, first hand, compiled by Grieve Mourne · **Days:** 1 to 5 · **Place:** Ashfall Hold

They came up out of the south on one cart and four backs, on a day that Sila Carver recorded as fair and did not trust, and they stopped on the low rise above the fields because Oda Ashfall stopped walking.

The cart was a farm cart with a cracked hub, and what was on it was this: two coulters and a share off two ploughs, one of which was broken; a crate of iron scrap that Ketil Brack had been collecting for nine years the way other men collect grievances; four hides; seed barley in two sacks, one of it good; a box of Sila Carver's tools; and one pane of glass wrapped in fleece, which nobody had asked about since the second valley and which Oda Ashfall would not put down.

There were fifteen of them. I am going to set them out here the way a Teller sets out a lineage, because they are the whole of it, and because everything this book is about was already standing on that rise in the afternoon of the first day, wanting its supper.

Oda Ashfall, who chose the ground. Rin Ashfall, her daughter, nineteen. Wren Ashfall, her younger sister, who carried the blade ninth. Maren Verren, who was Rin's wife and who carried it second. Sila Carver, who kept this from the beginning and cut the first pane. Bran Holt, who kept it after her and who wrote down nothing he was not asked for. Yeva Holt. Colm Grey. Ketil Brack, smith, and Bel Brack, who was his apprentice and his sister's son. Ketil Dunn, and Orrin Dunn his son, who had the eastern furlong off Bran Holt and carried the blade eighth. Sable Kettle, out of Low Kettle, whose people were already under the water. Nix Ember. Asha Vane, who was then fourteen and who kept the third hand on her knee inside a Warden's kit.

Of the fifteen, four are alive as I cut this line. Eight of them carried the blade.

The rise was chosen for three reasons and Oda Ashfall gave all three out loud, which is a thing she did and which the people who came after her did much less. Water on two sides, so that the ditching is done for you. Fall-ash four fingers down under a thin mat of grass, which Sable Kettle went and confirmed with her thumb before anybody unloaded anything. And sight of the fields, all the way out to the last fence and the long grass beyond it, because Oda Ashfall said she wanted to be able to see the grass move.

There was already a grave on the rise.

It was small and it was set square to nothing, and it had a cairn on it of round stones that had been carried up from the lake, which is a long way to carry round stones. Sable Kettle knew whose it was before she got to it. It was a child out of Low Kettle, brought up here by her own people and laid in the ground on the high side of the water before the water closed over the rest of them, because it was the nearest ground that was going to stay dry.

They did not move it and they did not build on it. Bran Holt wanted the Hearth on that exact spot for the draw and was told no, once, by Oda, in one word, and went eleven paces east and built it there instead, which is why the Hearth at Ashfall Hold faces the way it faces and why the door is in the awkward wall.

Sila Carver wrote the grave down on the fourth day under materials, because she had nowhere else to put it and because a Carver writes down what is on the site.

· · ·

The Hearth went up on the first day in the sense that the fire was in it before dark. Everything else about it took eleven days.

What went up on the first day was this. They cut sods off the rise with two spades and a hay knife, and stood them in rows to dry, and used the wet ones. They took stone off the walls of an old byre a hundred paces down the slope, which was standing to shoulder height with no roof on it and had animal bone in the floor of it and which nobody in the Hold ever spoke about again; Ketil Dunn and Orrin Dunn carried that stone up between them on a hurdle all afternoon and did not stop when it rained. They laid a footing without mortar, because there was no lime and would not be lime until the kilns at Stonewake were drawn, which happened in the second month. They built to knee height in the first afternoon, and to hip height on the second day, and the walls of the Hearth are still that stone up to hip height and dressed stone above it, and you can see the join from inside, and I have put my hand on the join.

The flue was Ketil Brack's. A smith knows draw the way a Carver knows plumb, and he built that flue with his forearms in it to the elbow, feeling the throat of it, taking a stone out and turning it and putting it back, for the better part of the second day, and he would not let anybody help him and he would not say why. When they lit it the smoke went up and stayed up and did not come back into the room in any wind, and it has never come back into the room in any wind. It is the single best piece of work in Ashfall Hold and the man who did it was twenty-six years old and had never built a chimney.

The hearthstone came out of the water.

That was Sable Kettle. She told them on the first evening that Low Kettle had a mill on the outflow and that the upper stone of the mill was above the water at the low end of the summer, and she was right, and on the second morning five of them walked down to the mere with poles and a hide rope. They were in to the waist for most of a morning. The stone was cracked across and they brought it up cracked and laid it crack side down, and it is the floor of the fire at Ashfall Hold, and everything the Hold has ever cooked has been cooked on the top stone of a drowned village's mill.

Nobody said anything about that on the second day. They were busy, and it was a good stone, and it was free.

I have gone looking for somebody who remembers anybody saying anything about it, and I have found two people who were there. Asha Vane says Sable Kettle sat down on the bank afterwards with her boots off and cried for a while and then got up and went back to work and nobody went over to her. Ketil Brack says nothing of the kind happened, that Sable Kettle was fourteen years old and laughing, and that Asha Vane has spent thirty years putting feeling into that morning because Asha Vane was eight and frightened. They were both on the bank. I record what was said. I do not settle it.

· · ·

On the first night there were three fires: one at the stone face, one at the sod rows, and one at the cart.

On the second night there were two, because the only dry wood was at the wood edge and that is two hours out and two hours back and nobody would spend a working day on it.

On the third day Oda Ashfall said one.

The reasons she gave were fuel and draw. One fire is easier to keep than three and does not need somebody sitting with it, and Ketil Brack backed her because a smith hates a wasted fire the way a Carver hates a wasted board. That is the whole of the reasoning that was said out loud on the third day and it is the whole of what Sila wrote down.

The other reason is a thing the Hold found out later and then never tested, because by the time anybody thought of testing it the rule was a rule. Things came to the outer fires twice in the first month. Nothing has ever come to the Hearth. Not once, in two hundred and ten days, and not once before that either as far as anybody can establish, and I have asked. The Hold has one fire because of wood and because of draw, and it has kept one fire for two hundred and ten days because of the other thing, and nobody in the Hold has ever put those two sentences next to each other in public.

· · ·

The jobs were said on the third day, in the morning, in the rain, with everybody standing.

There are six and there have only ever been six. Field-hand, Smith, Forager, Warden, Healer, Carver. They were not titles and nobody was proud of one. They were the answer to the question of who is doing what while the rest of us do the other thing, and Oda Ashfall said them off the top of her head in the order she thought of them, and that order is the order they are still said in at every Succession, because Sila wrote it down in that order and once a thing is written down in an order it has an order.

Bran Holt, Ketil Dunn, Orrin Dunn, Yeva Holt, Nix Ember and Rin Ashfall to the fields, and Bran Holt to apportion the furlongs, which he did that week with a knotted cord and no favour to anybody, including himself. Ketil Brack to the Smithy with Bel Brack under him. Sable Kettle Forager, which nobody argued with, because she had already put her thumb in the ground twice and told them the truth about it both times. Wren Ashfall Healer, which nobody wanted, because being the Healer means sitting still in a Hold where everybody else is carrying stone, and Wren took it without being asked twice and was better at it than anyone had a right to expect. Sila Carver and Asha Vane to the Carvers' shed, which was not a shed yet and was a tarpaulin over the cart.

And the Wardens.

Both Rin Ashfall and Colm Grey wanted it. Rin wanted it because she was nineteen and it was the one job with a fence in it. Colm Grey wanted it because Colm Grey had opinions about what was in the long grass and had already walked out to the last fence twice on his own without telling anybody, which is the first thing anyone in this Chronicle ever did on their own without telling anybody.

Oda gave it to Colm Grey and to Maren Verren, and gave Rin the fields.

Rin said there was nothing out there to fight.

Oda said that was what she was counting on, and that a Warden walks the fence and looks at grass, and that Rin would not last a week looking at grass.

Colm Grey said, in front of everyone, that he did not need a second Warden and particularly not that one. Maren Verren said nothing at all. Sila's entry for the third day is *jobs said, 6, argmnt abt Wardn, no blood*, which is the first joke in the Chronicle of Ashfall Hold and I do not think she knew she was making one.

Eight days later Rin Ashfall was carrying the only sword in the valley and Colm Grey was still walking the fence, and he walked it for seventy-four days after that, and then he took the blade third. What Colm Grey wanted on the third day he got on the eighty-fifth, at the price the book records, and I have never been able to decide whether that is a thing worth writing down or only a shape.

· · ·

The hand this chapter is in is a physical object and it is worth describing, because everything anybody knows about the first twenty days comes through it and it was made under conditions.

Sila Carver wrote on wax. Four boards, a hand and a half by two hands, recessed and filled, in a fold of hide with the stylus in a loop. Carvers carry them for materials: sods, boards, nails, hours, what the day used and what the day still owes. The wax was a finite quantity. There was no more wax in the valley and would not be any until somebody found bees or rendered enough fat to be careless with, so every stroke Sila Carver cut was cut against a stock she could see going down, and she cut accordingly. She dropped the vowels out of the middle of words. She cut *weather* as three strokes and a hook. She used one mark for the Hearth and another for the fields and a third for the mere, and I have had to learn those the way you learn a stranger's dialect, from repetition and from being wrong.

And she wrote down the weather every single day, first, before anything else, and she wrote it down with suspicion. *Fair & I do nt trust it.* *Fair agn. Wrng for the month.* *Rain in the ngt, not much, we wanted more.* Bran Holt, who came after her, did not record the weather at all unless it stopped work, and I have gone through his seventy-six days looking for the sky and found it four times. I resent that more than I can put a reason to.

The board went up on the fourth day. Sila planed a plank, one of the four good ones off the cart, and set it on two pegs in the wall of the Hearth to the right of the fire where the light falls on it in the afternoon, and it stayed blank for five days except for the rota, which she wrote on it in charcoal because charcoal wipes off and she was not going to cut a rota.

That is the board. Everything that is now on it, all four numbers, and the eighteen verbs, and the line at the bottom, went onto a plank that a Carver planed to write down who was carrying water.

· · ·

The fourth day had no weather in it.

It rained from before light and it was the kind of rain you do not work stone in, so all fifteen of them were inside the Hearth from dark to dark, which had not happened before and did not happen again for a long while. They mended things. They argued about the name of the place. Bel Brack won three times at a game with stones and was accused of something.

When they came out in the morning the sods they had cut and stood to dry on the second day were exactly as wet as they had been. The dew was in the places the dew had been in. The grass they had trodden flat on the path to the byre had not sprung up at all, not a blade of it. The bread was not stale. A pot of milk that had been standing since the third day had not turned. And the cut on the heel of Bel Brack's hand, which he had got off the stone face and which Wren Ashfall had looked at and told him to keep clean, had not scabbed over and had not closed and had not done anything, and he held it up and showed it to people, and it was the same cut.

Ketil Brack said the day had not been took off them.

Oda Ashfall said then they would spend fewer, and went to look at the ditch.

That is all. Nobody was frightened. I want to be accurate about the temperature of this, because it has become a horror since and it was not one then: they had been working for four days on short sleep and they had been given a day back, and a person who has been given a day back does not interrogate it. Sila Carver wrote it down under weather, which is where she wrote everything she did not have a category for, and because she wrote it under weather it sat there for eleven days doing nothing at all.

Bran Holt told me, once, sitting where I am sitting, that he had worked out what the fourth day meant before anybody else and had said nothing because nobody asked him. Asha Vane's note in the margin of that page says Bran Holt worked out nothing whatever, and that he was told what the fourth day meant on the fifteenth, in the yard, by Maren Verren, in front of Asha herself, and that his face did what it did. I record what was said. I do not settle it.

· · ·

The fifth day was fair and they set the doorstone.

Oda Ashfall would not have a new one cut. Sila had a good flag off the byre floor squared and ready and Oda went past it and down the slope and came back with two men and the byre's threshold stone, which was worn in the middle in a long shallow dip by somebody else's feet over somebody else's years, and she had that set at the Hearth door with the dip in it facing up, and stood on it, and said it would do.

Sila's entry for the fifth day is *Fair. Doorstone set. Od. Ashfl wld nt have a new one.* There is nothing after that sentence. It is the last thing Sila Carver wrote about Oda Ashfall while Oda Ashfall was a person you could hand a cup to.

Two testimonies say Oda named the place that evening and that she named it for the ash and not for herself, and that the family name and the ash were the same word by accident and she thought that was funny. A third says the name came off Sila's wax three days later, where she had been writing a short mark for the site all along, and that the mark was for ash fall because that is what was under the site, and that a place is called what the person with the stylus calls it. I record what was said. I do not settle it. I will add only that the second account frightens me more than the first, and that I have been the person with the stylus for seventy days.

In the last of the light she went in and got the pane out from under the bench and unwrapped it.

It was a single sheet of glass a hand and a half square, greenish at the edge where it thickened, with a bloom on one face that would not come off with spit and cloth. It had come out of a house that was not hers, in a valley she did not talk about, and she had carried it across two valleys on a cart with a cracked hub and had not let anybody else touch it. It was uncut. There was not a mark on it. Nobody in the Hold had ever asked her what it was for, and Sila Carver, who wrote down everything, did not write down what it was for either, which I take to mean that Oda Ashfall had not said.

She turned it once to the fire, and looked through it at the fire, and put it back in the fleece, and put it back under the bench.

She had one day left.
