# Chapter 17: The Long Hold and the Open Road

> *Six characters cut into a post at a crossing. A hold this way is keeping a board you can write on.*
> Recorded: the route-mark at the south-west corner of the Hold, six strokes, cut by a Wayfarer whose name is on the back of a leaf I do not have

**Hand:** Bran Holt · **Days:** 25 to 26 · **Place:** the palisade, the Vane Road

Three Wayfarers came down the Vane Road on the morning of the twenty-fifth and stood at the south-west corner of the palisade and called up.

I was at the Smithy door cutting the third verb onto it and I heard them before I saw them, because they were talking the way people talk when they have walked a long way and are at the end of it, which is to say they were talking about nothing, about the road, about whether the road was longer than it was last season, about who had been at the post and who had scored through the six characters on it. They had not been at the post. They were at the corner. They were looking up at me.

The post is at the corner of the palisade, at the foot of it, half a man's height, and the six characters are cut into it in a hand that is not Bran Holt's hand and not Sila Carver's hand and not the Hold's hand, and they are the first thing a person from outside sees when they come down the road. Six characters is the route-mark. It means: a hold this way is keeping a board you can write on. I have not known why six until I have asked the three Wayfarers standing at my post and got two answers and a refusal to answer, and the two answers were not the same answer, and one of them was that six was the number of days it took to walk between the holds that first agreed on the mark and the other was that six was the number of verbs they could fit on the back of a folded leaf, and I record what was said.

The six characters had been scored through with a single cut. The cut was not the cut of the characters. It was shallower at the start and ran out at the end, which is what a knife does when the hand using it is in a hurry. I asked the three Wayfarers about the score and got two answers and a refusal, and the two answers were not the same answer, and neither of the people named was in a position to have done it.

The three Wayfarers were thin. Two of them had the same build and the same gait and the same boots with the heels worn down on the outside, which is the build of a person who has been walking for years, and the third was a woman a hand shorter than the other two and lighter on her feet and she was not looking at me, she was looking at the palisade.

I said the Hold had room for the night.

·  ·  ·

I am Bran Holt and I am the second hand and I have just written on the page a thing for which there is no verb, and I am going to spend this chapter on the absence of that verb because Grieve Mourne has been compiling my hand for seventy days and has been writing the absence of that verb in the margins of every chapter that contains a Wayfarer and has been right to do it.

There are eighteen verbs on the board and not one of them is for opening the gate. There is `enemy_spared` and `enemy_slain` and `foreign_echo_honoured` and `foreign_echo_slain` and `echo_sent` and `ally_revived` and `ally_abandoned` and none of them, none of them, is the verb for what happened at the gate on the twenty-fifth day of the Hold when I said the Hold had room for the night and meant it and opened it.

There is no verb for hospitality. There is no verb for refusing it. A Hold can open its gate to a stranger every day for twenty years and the board will read The Quiet Field every morning. A Hold can bar its gate to a stranger every day for twenty years and the board will read The Quiet Field every morning. The board does not know what hospitality is. The board does not know what cruelty is. The board knows only what got written into it, and what gets written into it is what the verbs will hold.

Grieve Mourne has been pointing this out for the length of a book. He says the absence of the verb is the largest single fact about the Vale's political history. He says it is the reason a Hold can be hospitable and the next Hold can be cruel and they can have the same standing on the board. He says it is the reason the board cannot answer the question the Long Hold asks, which is whether a Hold that opens its gate to nobody is a Hold that has chosen something or a Hold that has refused to be chosen for.

I am going to spend the rest of my hand not answering him.

·  ·  ·

What the three Wayfarers actually carried was not what I had expected, and what I had expected was maps and what they carried was entries.

I am going to say that again because it is the only piece of knowledge in this chapter that has done any good in the world.

What the Wayfarers carried was entries. Ledger lines, copied out of other valleys' chronicles, on folded leaves of vellum and waxed boards and once on the back of a piece of bark that had been written on with a stick of lead. They had twenty-three leaves between them. They had four boards. They had the bark. They had copies out of six other valleys and they could read me what those valleys had done on the same days we had done what we had done, and the readings did not agree, and the disagreement was not because one of them was wrong but because the days were different days.

They will read you somebody else's day for a meal. That is the Open Road's price and it is not a price because the meal is not the point. The point is the day. The point is that your death on the fourteenth of your month is the same verb on somebody else's board on a different fourteenth of a different month in a different valley, and the verb carries the same four numbers and the numbers are the same numbers and the arithmetic is the same arithmetic, and a Hold that knows this is a Hold that has more in it than a Hold that does not.

I did not know this on the twenty-fifth. I know it now, and Grieve Mourne has known it since the seventy-fourth day of his hand, which is the day the first stranger's Echo arrived in the Vale and was buried, and the board moved, and the people who moved it were not in this valley when it moved, they were in another valley, and the line on their board said so.

That is the Open Road doctrine. It is the doctrine of a people who carry copies of chronicles between holds and bury strangers' Echoes for nothing. Their argument is that hold does not recognise a palisade so neither should a rite. Their weakness is that they bring other valleys' dead with them, and other valleys' arguments.

·  ·  ·

The Long Hold is the doctrine of the opposite.

The name comes from Vane Hold. Vane Hold is on the Vane Road, which is the road I am standing on. Vane Hold is a wall, four hundred paces of level course with no seam you can feel, set into the road at the head of a valley south of ours, and Vane Hold is not occupied, and Vane Hold has not been occupied for as long as the Wayfarers can remember, and the gate is shut, and the gate is shut from the inside, and the timber on the inside of the gate is two arms' worth of squared oak in iron brackets set into the stone, and the wall was built by a hold that was still trying and nobody now living could build it again.

The Long Hold is named after Vane Hold because Vane Hold is what a Long Hold leaves. A wall. A gate. A barred gate from the inside. A way of holding the line that worked and is now a ruin on a road.

The Wayfarer who told me about Vane Hold is the third one, the woman a hand shorter than the other two. She said she had been to Vane Hold twice. She said the first time she had walked round the wall and the second time she had stood at the gate and listened and had not heard anything from inside it. She said the gate was barred when she arrived and barred when she left. She said she had not lifted the timber.

I am writing her name here for the first time, and I am writing it down flatly and with no weight, because she is a walk-on in this chapter and she will not be a walk-on for long and the chapter has not yet decided how much to tell.

Tove Rowan.

She is a Wayfarer, from a hold two ridges east, and she keeps the open road because the open road is what her hold does, and her hold is the only hold I have yet heard of that keeps no Chronicle of its own. They carry leaves in a chest and copy what other valleys have done and they do not commit to a board, and she has said this to me as if it were a fact about the weather and not a position in an argument, and I have not yet decided whether she is the most honest person I have met or the most dangerous.

She did not stay the second night. She left before dawn on the twenty-sixth with one of the three leaves and a copy of our board up to the day of her arrival, and the leaf she took was the leaf with the smear on it, which I had not shown her and which she had not asked for.

I did not stop her.

·  ·  ·

The Long Hold is what the wall on the Vane Road stands for, and the Open Road is what Tove Rowan stands for, and the Hold that lets the three of them in is what I stood for on the morning of the twenty-fifth when I said the Hold had room for the night and meant it, and the board does not know about any of it because there is no verb.

There are four readings in this book and they are all of them correct. The Tellers and the Unnaming and the Hearthkeepers and the Cull and the Wayfarers and the Long Hold and the Gravewardens and the Yielding are all correct readings of a real problem. The board does not average them. The board does not say which of them is right. The board says what the board says, which is the four numbers and the fifth line and the name of an ending, and the ending is whatever the four numbers drift toward, and the four numbers drift toward what the Hold has done, and what the Hold has done is what the verbs will hold, and the verbs will not hold hospitality, and the absence of that verb is the largest single fact about the Vale's political history, and Grieve Mourne is right, and I am going to bed.

The route-mark at the south-west corner has been re-cut. The single score is still on the post, under the new six. I did not scrape it out. I cut beside it. The score is from somebody in a hurry, and the six is from somebody on the road, and a post with both is a post with a history, and the post has earned the history.

Tove Rowan left the leaf with the smear on it. I do not know what she did with it. I have not asked her. I have asked the other Wayfarers about the leaf and they say they do not know about the leaf and they may be lying.

I am Bran Holt and I am the second hand and I have opened a gate for which there is no verb and I have watched a woman walk out with a smear I did not give her and I have re-cut the route-mark at the foot of the palisade and I have recorded what was said.

---

Grieve Mourne, compiling. I have this leaf in front of me in the fourth hand. Tove Rowan is in it for the first time. She is in it three more times after this, and the chapter after this is the one where the smear is on her copy and a pane in her valley has the smear in it, and she never tells me where she got it, and I never ask, and the leaf has been in two books.

The route-mark at the south-west corner has been re-cut twice since Bran Holt cut it and the second cut was mine and the score under it is now the deepest thing on the post.
