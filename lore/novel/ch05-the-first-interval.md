# Chapter 5: The First Interval

> *D8. Fair agn, wrng for month. K. Brck wnt to W fld at last lght & cme bck up on his own ft. Stake brke. Hv kept shrt half.*
> Sila Carver, first hand, dated eighth day, cut on the ninth with the rest

**Hand:** Sila Carver, disputed by two villager testimonies
**Days:** 8 to 9
**Place:** Wrenfield, Ashfall Hold

Bran Holt had said in the middle of the day that nobody was going at her. Ketil Brack had not answered him. At the last of the light on the eighth day Ketil Brack took a stake off the stook, thorn, five foot, pointed at one end by his own hand two evenings before, and went down the headland into the west field.

He told the Hold afterward what he had gone down to do, and he told it the same way three times, which is worth something. He said he had not gone down to break her. He said he had gone down to put her over. A thing that is put on the ground and gets up again is one problem, and a thing that is put on the ground and stays there is a different problem, and the Hold had fourteen people in it and needed to know which of the two it had before it spent anything on either.

He came in on her left, which is the side the right hand swings away from. He waited for the fourth pass. She had done nine hundred of them by then at least, and at the end of every fourth she straightened and put her right hand out to the side at hip height and drew the edge of nothing along a cloth that was not there, twice, once each way, and that was the longest she was ever still. He had stood on the headland most of the day and counted it. He came in on the count, and he came in behind her, and he was right about the count.

He hit her across the back of the shoulders with the flat of the stake, two-handed, the way a man drives a wedge. He is a Smith. The Hold has never since had anybody who could put more into a thing with a stick.

He said the sound was wrong. That is the first thing he ever said about it and it is the thing he was still saying about it eighty days later. He said it was the sound of a beetle coming down on a stake that is already home in the ground, no give in it anywhere, and the blow came back up the thorn into his wrists and his elbows and he felt it arrive in the back of his own neck.

She did not go off her feet. She did not go forward onto her hands. Her shoulders did not drop. The count did not break. The left hand went out and closed on nothing and drew nothing in to the hip, and the right hand came across low, and the weight went from the right foot to the left, and the mark of the stake was across her shoulders while she did it.

He hit her twice more, lower, at the short ribs on the left, and into the second of those he put his whole back. The stake broke about a foot above his hands.

Then the fourth pass came round.

The right hand went out to the side at hip height, in the flat arc, at the height and the reach it had gone out at all day, and Ketil Brack was standing in it.

It took him across the outside of the left knee and the shin. A hand and a forearm with no iron in either. He went off his feet sideways and came down on his right hip and his right shoulder in the stubble with the wind out of him, and he lay in the swathe for the length of two more passes, which is to say that she gathered nothing twice and laid nothing down twice while he was on the ground beside her.

Then he got up. He got himself up without using the arm he had come down on, and he walked out of the field and up the headland, and he was over the top of it before Wren Ashfall reached him.

What he said, in the Smithy, sitting on the anvil block with his side strapped, was that a man wants to be heavier before he goes at one.

He said it twice that night. Sila Carver's hand has it once.

The Wardens have a word for it now. They say poise, the way a Carver says plumb, and they say it flat and they say it often, and they teach it as two things that are the same thing: what it takes to open a body that is carrying enough, and what it takes to stand up under a body that has already agreed to its own swing. Ketil Brack learned both halves of that in about nine heartbeats. He learned them as a bruise across the knee, two ribs cracked and not gone, and a broken piece of thorn. No number came out of it. There was no number in the Vale that night for one to come out of.

He had made two mistakes and he named them both himself, which is why I have his account and not somebody's guess at it. The first was not a mistake. He came in on the fourth pass because the fourth pass was the longest she was still, and that is what a Warden does now and what a Warden is taught to do first. The second was that he stood where the cloth hangs. He had watched her reach out to that one place in the air two hundred times between morning and dark, and he stood in it. When Bran Holt put it to him he said he had been counting her arc and not her hands.

The Wardens teach both now and they teach the hands first.

He took the short half of the stake back to the Smithy and looked at the break by firelight for some time. Thorn breaks in a long splinter and this had broken square across, which he said he had seen happen to a handle that has been left standing in water, and this stake had been pointed at his own fire two days before and had been dry. He put the piece on the shelf over the bellows. It sat there for the rest of his life. Sila Carver kept the other half in the Chronicle chest and it is still in it, and it is the oldest object in this Hold that anybody put away on purpose.

Wren Ashfall strapped him with sacking and a boiled cloth and told him he would be no use at the bellows for a fortnight. He was at the bellows before the ninth day was out.

·  ·  ·

Bran Holt went down into the field after dark to bring the broken stake out, not knowing Ketil had taken it, because it was good thorn and pointed and the Hold had forty-one of them and needed all forty-one.

He walked out from her on the way back, heel to toe, counting, and at three paces the ground changed under him.

Inside the three paces the stubble was flat and bent inward, all of it leaning in toward where she stood, which is not what a foot does to stubble and not what weather does to it either. He pulled a handful there and a handful at twenty paces off and carried both up to the Hearth and laid them on the boards by the fire with the roots toward the light, side by side, and told people to come and look, and people came and looked.

The roots from twenty paces off were the colour a root is.

The roots from inside the three paces were white. Not dry and not rotten. Wet white, the white at the bottom of a leek, and the whole of the root pale like that and not only the tip, and no smell to them, and nothing on them.

Sable Kettle put both handfuls in her palm and weighed them against each other and then did the thing she does, which is put her thumb into the soil that came up with them and work it against her fingers. She had done that on the first day before anybody unloaded the cart and she had been right about the ground then. She said the ash was the same ash. She said the ash out of the three paces was the same ash as the ash out of twenty paces off, the same colour and the same feel between the fingers, and that whatever had happened to the roots had not happened to the ground they were in.

She was wrong about that, or she was right about it and the Vale has since learned to mean something else by it. She was fourteen years old and she was the Forager and it was the eighth day and nobody knew anything. What she said stands in Sila's hand as she said it and I have not corrected it. There is a Forager's test now and it takes longer than a thumb.

Bran Holt stepped the ring again at first light on the ninth day and got five paces, and cut three and five on the back of the Field-hands' stick with the day between them.

The Tellers write now that the ring stands at three paces on the first day and eleven by the fourth. The Hold never had a fourth day with Oda Ashfall. That number came into the Vale from somewhere and I have not found where, and I have looked in four other valleys' copies for it, and I am not going to supply it out of my own head to make this page tidy.

·  ·  ·

That night the Hold argued in the Hearth with the door shut, fourteen people and the fire made up twice, and every position the Vale has held since was spoken in that room by somebody with a job.

Colm Grey said leave her. He was one of the two Wardens and he had stood on the headland eight hours and had the best count of anybody. He said she had not been out of three paces in a day and a night, she was cutting nothing the Hold ate, and the Hold had fourteen people and a third of a palisade. Put the fence on the far side of her, he said, and put the labour into stakes. He did not say it cruelly and nobody in the room heard it cruelly. It was the cheapest thing anybody said all night. Bran Holt costed it out loud at three half-days and every person in that room heard the number and knew what three half-days was worth to them that week.

Ketil Dunn said fire. He came out of country where the dead go into fire, and he said so, and his reason was one sentence: nothing that has been burned comes back up a field. He was the only person in the room with a reason that had been tested somewhere. He could not say what it had been tested against. He said that too.

Sable Kettle said water, and everybody in the room knew what she was saying. Her people are in Kettlemere. She had walked into that mere to the waist on the second morning to bring the hearthstone up off the old mill and she had gone back to work afterward, and the accounts of what she did on the bank between those two things do not agree and I have set both down elsewhere. What she said on the eighth day was that her people put them in the water. Then she said that she did not know what water did to this, and that neither did anybody in that room, and that a thing you cannot walk out to is a thing you cannot take back. Then she said nothing else for the rest of the night.

That is the first time water was raised in Ashfall Hold. It was raised and put down inside a minute by the same person. The Vale has never had a rite for water. There is a village under that lake and there is a board in this Hearth that has never had a line on it about them, and I will come back to that on a later page and it will not read any better there.

Yeva Holt, who is Bran Holt's daughter, and whom Colm Grey called sister when he had drink in him, which the Hold never settled and which I am not going to settle, said wall her. Hurdles round the three paces, eight of them, a day's work, then leave it and go on with the palisade. Her father costed that one too and then said the thing that killed it: a fence wants mending for as long as it stands, and a Hold of fourteen cannot keep a fence it does not eat from. He said it to his own daughter in front of everybody and she took it. I have set both halves of that down because the Vale got an answer to that argument later, on the Vane Road, and the answer was not the one Bran Holt gave.

Wren Ashfall said that whatever was in the west field was her sister, who had been failed, by the slope, by the stake, and by her own two hands on the wound, and that it was owed something, and that the something was not a beating. She said take her, keep her where she cannot walk, feed the Hold in the meantime, and find out what is owed. Bran Holt asked how long finding out would take. She said she did not know.

Ketil Brack said break her. He said it from the floor with his side strapped, because he could not sit on the bench and breathe at the same time, and he said it in short sentences for the same reason. He said she was harder on the eighth day than she had been on the seventh and that he had felt it come back up the stake into his neck. He said he did not know what she would be on the fourth day after, or the tenth, and that nobody in the room did either. Break her now, he said, while she is three paces wide and one woman and cheap. He was the only person in that Hearth who had put a hand on her. The Cull say now that he was right, and they say it about this night in particular, and I have not found the argument that answers him. I have been looking for it for seventy days.

Maren Verren said nothing at all. She was in the room the whole night. She was the other Warden. Three accounts put her at the end of the bench by the door and not one of them has her speaking.

Rin Ashfall said she wanted her mother in the ground. That is not an argument and nobody in the room took it as one. It is what won.

·  ·  ·

It was full dark and the fire had been made up twice when somebody in that Hearth said three words in the order the Vale has said them in ever since.

*The loosing. That one is done. Nobody did it and nobody can undo it.*

*The holding. That is where we are. It is closed and it is nobody's.*

*The keeping. That is ours, and we have not done it.*

They are not holy words and they were never meant to be. You loose a beast off its tether. A holding is ground you have and have not settled anything about. You put hay in the keeping and grain in the keeping, and to keep a thing is to store it so that it lasts and so that it can be got at again by whoever comes to the store after you. Fourteen people in a smoky room found that they already had three words for what to do with a thing that was not theirs yet, and they used them, and the Vale has said them in that order at every rite it has held since, and I have heard them said in two other valleys by people who have never heard of Oda Ashfall.

Sila Carver wrote one line about it the next day.

*We found the words in the night.*

She wrote we. I have been asked to make that a single person eleven times, four of those by Tellers who wanted the line clean for a Telling, and I have not done it.

The first testimony is Ketil Brack's, taken from his own mouth by the third hand on the ninety-seventh day and copied here in Asha Vane's writing:

*I said them. I was on the floor with my side strapped and could not get breath for more than a short sentence, so I said them short, three of them, and short is why they lasted. Nobody else in that room had put a hand on her. I had. A man who has been on the ground gets to name the ground.*

Ketil Brack was on the floor. A man on the floor remembers a room from the floor, and the floor is the one place in a room from which you cannot see who else is speaking.

The second testimony is Sable Kettle's, given to me at this Hearth in the last season she had, when she was carrying the knife and had a fortnight left:

*It was Rin. She said them standing, by the door, and she said them about her own mother, and that is why they stayed said. Ketil Brack talked all night. Rin Ashfall talked once.*

Rin Ashfall was dead on the fourteenth day. Whatever she said on the eighth she said six days before anybody in this valley had a reason to write down who had said anything. She was never asked. She cannot be asked. Both of the accounts I have were kept a long time in the heads of people who had a share in the night, and one of the two was taken down by me, and I am in this and will not pretend otherwise.

Bran Holt's hand, which is the fullest hand this Chronicle ever had, says only that the words were said in the Hearth on the eighth day and that they were good words.

I record what was said. I do not settle it.

·  ·  ·

The other number the Vale got out of that night was nine, and it came into the room twice from two directions, and I cannot tell which of the two it came out of.

Ketil Brack brought it in as a reason to hurry. Where he came from they reckoned strays at nine days, and he said the saying, which is that a beast loose nine days belongs to whoever has been feeding it. He said the ground out there had been feeding her for two.

Bran Holt brought it in at the end of the evening by accident. He cut the eighth day onto the stick and looked at what he had cut and said out loud, to nobody, that the Hearth would be nine days old in the morning and had put nobody in the ground yet.

The Vale has said nine days ever since and means by it too long. What it counts nine days from depends entirely on who you ask. The Wardens count nine days from the loosing and will tell you so shortly. The Carvers count nine days from the day you first could have done the thing you have not done, which makes the number older and worse. Both trades will tell you the other one is confused, and both have been saying so at the turn of every season since, and the number has not moved.

The Hold in fact waited three days. That is what the board says and the board is right. Nine is the measure and three is what happened, and no hand has ever explained why the measure is not the thing it measures.

·  ·  ·

At first light on the ninth day Sila Carver went to the trestles under the tarpaulin and stood there for a while, and then went down the slope to the byre.

The long spade was in the corner of it where somebody had left it standing, and had been standing there when they took the walls down to hip height for the Hearth footing, and nobody had moved it because nobody had needed it. It had a shoulder on the blade for a boot and an ash haft worn to a hand, and the hand was not anybody's in Ashfall Hold. Ketil Brack put a nail through the shoulder in the middle of the ninth day without being asked.

She carried it up the slope and past the Hearth door, and eleven paces west of that door, on the high side of the rise where the round stones were, she stood at the foot of a cairn that was already there when they came and paced off ground beside it, heel to toe, four by seven.

Then she came back down and asked for two people and a hurdle.

## Ledger

None. The three motions were named on the eighth day and no verb was cut for the naming of them, then or since. There are eighteen verbs and there have only ever been eighteen, and not one of them is for a thing said in a room.
