# Chapter 20: Low Kettle Under the Mere

> *I have entered the child. I have not entered the village. The board cannot hold what no
> hand can walk to, and I am writing it here so that whoever reads this knows the count is
> short.*
> Recorded: Bran Holt, second hand, in the margin of the thirty-eighth day

**Hand:** Bran Holt, second hand, with a Wayfarer copy from a hold on another water
**Days:** 33 to 38
**Place:** Kettlemere, the near shallows; the mere road

Low Kettle is under the mere. It was a village and it drowned, and it drowned before the
Hearth was raised, and nobody in Ashfall Hold on the thirty-third day had been in it since
they were children.

Its dead are in the Holding. They have been in the Holding longer than any dead anywhere
in this Chronicle and they are in it at a depth nobody can walk to. That is the fact the
whole of this chapter is arranged around, and I am going to state its consequence at the
start and again at the end, because I have read what this book does to people who only
meet it once.

The board cannot count what no Teller can survey. Not will not. Cannot. An entry needs a
day, a verb, a subject and four numbers, and a subject needs somebody to have gone and
looked. No hand has ever gone down. So Low Kettle has never been entered, and the count of
outstanding Echoes in this Chronicle has never included it, and the last line of the board
is read off that count.

The lake is not on the board. It was not on it on the thirty-eighth day and it is not on it
on the page I am writing this on.

·  ·  ·

There was one exception and her name was Bel Kettle and she was countable because somebody
carried her.

The path up out of Low Kettle came to the top of the rise where the Hearth stands now. When
the water came up, somebody carried the child up that path and laid her at the top of it,
where there were people, and where there was ground, and where the water at that hour was
not.

I have that from two people who were both eight years old at the time, which was forty
years before the Hearth was raised on the same rise, and they gave it to Bran Holt in the
same words years apart, which is either corroboration or a thing a valley tells itself. I
record what was said.

Then the water came up the path.

So the place where she was laid is in the mere now, under a chest of water, about ninety
paces out from the shell bank, and Ashfall Hold raised its Hearth two hundred paces
further up the same rise on ground that stayed dry, and the mere road runs along the top
of the shell bank between the two, and the mere road comes up to the north-east arc of the
palisade.

Everything in this valley is close to everything else. That is the whole of the Vale's
geography and most of its trouble.

She was the only name Low Kettle gave the board. Not because she was loved more than the
rest of them. Because a man walked uphill with her and other people watched him do it, so
there was a name and a place and a witness, and those three things are an entry and
nothing else is.

·  ·  ·

Her Echo had been standing in the near shallows since before day one.

The board's figure for it on the thirty-eighth day is thirty-eight days. That figure is
wrong and everybody who wrote it knew it was wrong. Thirty-eight is what the board could
say, because the board begins at the raising of the Hearth and counts the days the Vale
spent moving, and the water's own count is older than the counting. There is no entry for
what happened to Low Kettle for the same reason there is no entry for the nine old rings
in the Grey Ash that the Foragers have mapped and never walked into: it happened before
there was anybody to write it.

Bran Holt wrote thirty-eight and put a mark beside it. He used that mark four times in
seventy-six days. It means *this number is the best I have*.

Here is the thing that should have frightened the Hold from the first year and did not,
because it looked like good news.

She had not grown.

An Echo grows a tier every two days and at the third it leaves its ring and roams and at
the fifth it comes to the palisade. Yeva Holt's did all of that in six days and I have
written that chapter. This one had stood in a hand's depth to a chest's depth of water for
longer than the Hold had existed and it was at a growth the Foragers put at two, and two is
what a four-day ring in a barley field comes to.

The Foragers had an answer and it was the right one and nobody wanted it.

An Echo feeds out of the ground. It pulls hold out of the soil around the place the body
stopped and what it leaves behind is grey and loose and will not grow anything, and that
is drawn-ash, and telling drawn-ash from fall-ash is the whole of a Forager's craft. Her
ground was under a lake. There was no soil for her to draw on. So she took almost nothing
and she stored almost nothing and she stayed where she was for forty years.

The mere had been holding her. The mere is still holding the rest of them.

I am going to put that as plainly as I know how, and once, and then let it sit. The one
piece of luck this valley has had in two hundred and ten days is a body of standing water
that nobody in the valley made, nobody in the valley maintains, and nobody in the valley
can guarantee for a single season. Vane Hold barred a gate on a correct premise. Ashfall
Hold has been kept from the worst thing in its own ground by weather.

·  ·  ·

On the thirty-third day the water had dropped a hand's depth, as it does in a dry summer,
and Maren Verren went down to the shell bank with two Foragers to get shell for the
furrows, carrying four flasks, which nobody in this Vale had ever carried.

The ring was out of the water.

That is the whole event of the thirty-third day and it is the event the next five days come
out of. An Echo's ring bends the grass inward and pales it at the root, and in a mere it
bends the reeds inward and pales the shell, and for forty years the rim of Bel Kettle's
ring had been a circle of pale shell under a chest of water where nobody could see it. The
water went down a hand and about four paces of the rim came out onto dry sand at the
bottom of the bank, and the sand inside the line was grey and loose, and the sand outside
the line was not.

One of the two Foragers put a hand on the line and then took it off and did not say
anything about it for two days.

The mere road runs twenty paces above that sand.

·  ·  ·

The Hold argued for four days and the argument was not about whether. It was about how, and
there was no answer to how, because the Vale has no rite for water and has never had one.

Bran Holt wrote the five rites out in the Chronicle on the thirty-fifth day with a line
under each of them. It is the most useful page in the second hand and it is four sentences
long.

```
Burial. A hole, the body, the gear. There is no hole under a lake.
Pyre. A fire and a floor. There is no fire under a lake.
Telling. Recounted aloud in full by someone who saw it. Nobody living saw Low Kettle go
    under except two who were eight.
Monument. Requires a reclaimed Echo and a relic and ground to set a stone on.
Wake. Requires sixty. The pool stands at twenty.
```

Every rite this Vale has ever performed assumes dry land and a body a person can reach.
The five of them are not a doctrine. They are five things that were invented by frightened
people in the first month and then worked, and every one of them was invented on a
hillside.

So the Hold could reach one of Low Kettle's dead, and could reach her only because a man
had carried her uphill forty years earlier, and could not reach any of the others, and had
no rite that would have covered them if it could.

Maren Verren said one thing in the four days. She said she would go in for the one she
could get to.

That is not a doctrine either. It is the only sentence in this chapter that anybody in it
could act on.

·  ·  ·

She went in on the thirty-eighth day at the middle of the morning, off the shell bank, with
the Widow's Maul and four flasks, and she knew the Maul was the wrong tool.

She said so at the bank. Two Wardens had put the case to her and the case was correct. In
water you want short and you want quick. A Hearth Knife is eighteen of reach and nine of
power and six hundredths of a second between deciding and the edge being where you decided,
and six hundredths is a thing that still exists under water.

Her answer is recorded in nine words. She said nine would not put down something that had
been standing since before the Hearth.

She was right and so were they, and this is the kind of disagreement the Vale has most of.

·  ·  ·

Here is what fighting in water is, as it was established on the thirty-eighth day and has
never needed establishing again, because after the thirty-eighth day nobody in Ashfall Hold
ever went into the mere for any reason at all.

The bottom is not ground. It is shell under silt and a foot goes into it to the ankle and
comes out slower than it went in. You cannot set your feet, which means you cannot put
your weight behind anything, which means everything you throw you throw with your arms
only.

At the ring it is chest deep, and chest deep is the worst depth there is. It is too deep to
stand in and too shallow to be carried by.

The moment anything moves the silt comes up. She fought in white water. She said afterwards
that she saw it twice in the whole of it and that both times were in the first minute.

The purse empties faster and the Wardens will tell you so and they are not being poetic
about it. You are holding yourself up and swinging at the same time and you are paying for
both, and the water takes the swing and does not give any of it back. A heavy swing with
the Maul takes forty-six hundredths of a second to arrive on dry ground. In chest-deep
water the head arrives late and arrives light and the striking face is dished, which Ketil
Brack did on purpose and which is right on a scarp and wrong in a lake, because a dished
face pushes a bow wave of water in front of it the whole way.

And there is no roll. That is the one nobody who has not done it understands. Every wielder
in this Chronicle has been kept alive at least once by the quarter second in which nothing
can touch you, inside the half second in which you can do nothing else. That quarter
second is a thing you buy by moving faster than what is around you. There is nothing to
buy it with in water. You can go slowly or you can go slowly.

She committed two heavy swings in the deep. The first one arrived and did nothing. The
second one she did not finish, because by then it was on her.

It did not have to come up.

·  ·  ·

It was small. It had no reach and no skill and it had died with nothing, and what it
brought to the thirty-eighth day was the water and forty years of standing in it and no
need to breathe.

It took her under and held her under. Bran Holt's entry does not say how long and the two
Foragers on the bank gave two different counts and I am not choosing between them.

She had four full flasks on her.

A flask is pyre-ash steeped and settled three days and drawn off clear and cut with
hearth-water, and what it does is close a grip that has come open, and it gives you back
the hold you have already spent. It is drunk. That is the whole of the method and there has
never been another. To drink it a body needs its head above water, and getting her head
above water was the one thing in the mere that was not available to her.

She was at the bottom of the purse with four charges on her belt. Every wielder who has
heard this told has understood it immediately and none of them has ever wanted to talk
about it afterwards.

What got her up was the short edge.

Ketil Brack ground a short edge into the trailing side of the Maul head in the Smithy in
the first month, without being asked, and he never explained why, and he was asked. In
water you cannot swing but you can pull, and she pulled the haft back through the water
toward her chest with both hands and the trailing edge came back with it, and it went in,
and the grip on her opened enough.

That is one of two times the short edge saved Maren Verren. It saves one other person, once,
on the two hundred and tenth day, and that person is me.

·  ·  ·

She came up and she went for the bank and it followed her.

She got into a hand's depth on the shell and onto her knees, and it came to the rim and
stopped.

They do not leave their ring until the third growth. Everybody in the Vale has known that
since the nineteenth day and it held here, and it held in a place where nobody had thought
to ask whether it would, and the rim it stopped at was the rim as the rim now was. Four
paces of pale shell on dry sand at the foot of the bank, where the water had been in the
spring and was not any more.

Bran Holt wrote that down in eleven words and did not comment on it. It is the first
observation anybody in this Vale ever made that a ring moves when its ground moves. It is
in the Chronicle, unexplained, and I have nothing to add to it, and there are two Foragers
in this valley now who will not walk the mere road in a dry summer.

She drank three flasks standing in a hand's depth of water with her back to the bank.

Then she went back in along the shell in the shallow, where she could set her feet, and it
came out to meet her into a hand's depth of water, because a hand's depth of water was
inside its ring now, and a Widow's Maul works perfectly well in a hand's depth of water.

She put it down in three.

I am not going to make that sentence longer. Bran Holt did not, and he was there on the
bank with the board on his knees, and he was fifty-one and he had a bad hip and he had
walked down for it because it was an entry.

·  ·  ·

The reeds inside the ring stood up in about the time it takes to draw breath and the pale
shell stopped where it was and has not moved in two hundred days.

What came back was a knife and one shoe.

The knife is a kitchen knife. Eighteen of reach, nine of power, six hundredths of a second
of decision edge. It hung on the wall at the Hearth from the thirty-eighth day and the
Hold cut bread with it for a hundred and three days, and then Sable Kettle took it off the
wall and carried nothing else and outlived three people who carried better. Whether she was
of Low Kettle's people is a thing this valley has an opinion about and the Chronicle does
not.

The shoe was a child's and it was the left one.

Bran Holt entered the shoe. I have read every hand in this Chronicle, including the one I
am writing with, and the second hand is the dull one, and it is the only one of the four
that would have entered the shoe.

·  ·  ·

The purse came out on the bank.

An Echo stores what the ground draws and the Hold could not spend, and when the grip opens
all of it arrives at once on whoever is standing there. Bran Holt had the board on his knees
and he had the figure before Maren Verren was out of the water.

The pool had stood at twenty since the thirty-first day. It stood at forty-one when she came
up the bank.

Twenty-one. That is what forty years under a lake comes to.

A four-day ring in second-season barley at Wrenfield came to forty-five, and every hand
since has read those two figures on facing pages and understood what the Foragers had been
saying about soil, and Bran Holt wrote beside the twenty-one that he did not understand the
difference and would not guess at it, which is the second hand at its best and its worst in
one line.

The grief for the child herself he entered at ten.

Ten is what any death costs and three is what every bond costs and he could count no bonds,
because every bond Bel Kettle had is under the water with nobody left to name them, and a
bond the board cannot count is a bond the board does not pay for. He wrote ten. He wrote in
the margin that the number was wrong. He did not write what he thought the right number was.

Nobody held a rite. Twenty-one and ten arrived and the pool sat at forty-one, and there is
no entry for a vigil on the thirty-eighth day because there was not one, and the reason
given at the time and written down was that the Hold was three days out from a burn in
Brackwood that wanted nine people in it and could not spare the days.

That is the next chapter and it is not about water.

·  ·  ·

The count of outstanding Echoes in this Chronicle goes to nothing on the thirty-eighth day
and stays at nothing until the eighty-fourth, with one day's exception on the seventieth
when a stranger's Echo walked into the Vale and was buried before dark. Forty-six days. It
is the longest clean stretch in the book. The board read The Quiet Field for fifteen more
of them and then said The Wake for the first time, and it said it because a wielder walked
past a Gravewarden, and that is another chapter and the Cull have never forgiven it.

The board was wrong about the nothing.

Low Kettle is under the mere. Its dead are in the Holding at a depth no Teller could ever
survey, so they were never entered, so the board never knew, so the last line of the board
has been read for two hundred and ten days off a count that is short by a village.

On the two hundred and tenth day the Hold declared a Wake. The Wake is the only one of the
five last pages that is gated, and what it is gated on is that no Echo is left standing, and
that gate was open on the board and I opened it, and I was standing in the shallows of
Kettlemere with a flask in me and Wren Ashfall's grief in the yard behind me when I worked
out what the board had never been able to say.

I wrote the line anyway. I have not settled it. I do not think it can be settled, and I do
not think that is the same as it being all right.

## The Other Roads

### Draining the mere

Cut the Kettle at the head, or breach the moraine at the low end, which two men with picks
could do in a season and which has been proposed in this valley three times.

The water goes. Low Kettle comes up. And what comes up is not a ruin. It is a village with
its dead in it, in the Holding, in a posture nobody has a word for, and the moment the
water is off them they have soil, and soil is what an Echo eats.

I have this from a Wayfarer copy made three valleys south, and it is eleven pages, and the
eleven pages are legible. They drained. The board's first entry afterwards is the one you
would expect and the numbers are the worst single line in any chronicle I have read.

`region_lost` the mere · Memory -4 · Mercy -7 · Communion -5 · Dominion -4

Seven off Mercy and five off Communion in one entry, and then the shell beds dry out and
the furrows stop getting shell, and a hold on ash ground that stops putting bone and lye
and shell back into its furrows has four rich seasons and then a famine on soil that looks
identical.

**Pressure:** toward **The Long Hold**, and it is the purest road to it in this book. Not
because draining a lake is cruel. Because a hold that has opened forty years of the Holding
onto its own doorstep never opens anything again, and Dominion goes up every time it
decides that, and Mercy goes down every time it acts on it, and fifty and minus twenty is
not a far walk from there.

### Sealing it and cutting no pane

The Unnaming's best case in this Chronicle, and I want it on the page in its strongest form
because the Unnaming are not a cult and the people who write them as one have not read
them.

Take the mere road off the rota. Work the far shore. Cut no pane for Bel Kettle, enter no
verb, let the thirty-eighth day be a day on which nothing happened. The dead of this Vale
did not walk before this Vale started writing them down, and here is a whole village that
has not walked in forty years, and the difference between that village and every field in
Wrenfield is that nobody ever wrote the village down or said its names out loud.

The water agrees with them. That is the part the Tellers have never had an answer for. Low
Kettle is the only large body of the dead in this Vale that has never once come to anybody's
palisade, and it is also the only one nobody has ever called by name.

`echo_abandoned` Bel Kettle · Memory -5 · Mercy -3 · Communion -2 · Dominion -1

**Pressure:** toward **Ash Inheritance**. Five off Memory an entry does not sound like much
and the road is not made of one entry. It is made of a hold that learns it can decline a
subject, and then declines the next one, and the panes go uncut in the order the names came
in, and Memory falls past minus forty, and what is at the bottom of that road is a chronicle
whose last forty pages are in one hand writing under a name that is not its own. I have that
copy. I have read it twice and I will not read it again.

### The hold that dived for all of them

This is the copy in the Hand line at the top of this chapter and it is the most important
document I have from outside this valley.

A hold on another water, four valleys west, with its own drowned village and rather more of
it than ours. They went down. Not once. As a standing practice, over what their board counts
as nineteen days and their own talk calls most of a generation, with weighted lines off a
raft and a knife apiece and no maul, because they had worked out in their first season what
Maren Verren worked out in one morning.

They got all of them. Every name. The entries are there, thirty-one of them, and they are
the same four numbers every time and they climb.

`echo_reclaimed` · Memory +6 · Mercy +3 · Communion +1 · Dominion +2, thirty-one times

`wielder_died` · Memory +2 · Mercy 0 · Communion 0 · Dominion -3, four times

Four wielders. Three of the four are entered as having been brought up, and the fourth
entry has no place of death in it, and their Teller wrote in the margin of that one that the
board had no line for a body nobody could reach, and that she was aware of the difficulty.

**Pressure:** toward **The Wake**, and this is the only road in this book that actually opens
that gate over water. The Wake wants Memory at forty-five and Communion at forty and no Echo
left standing, and thirty-one reclaimings will carry the first two on their own, and the
third one is the one that cannot be drifted into and cannot be fudged and cannot be got at
any price except the price they paid.

They ended on it. Their last page is a Wake and it is honest.

Ours is a Wake too. I have set the two of them side by side on my own table and read them
one after the other and there is nothing in either document that lets me say the two words
mean the same thing.
