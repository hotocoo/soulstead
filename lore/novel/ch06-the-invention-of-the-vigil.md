# Chapter 6: The Invention of the Vigil

> *D9. Fair. Od. Ashfl in grnd, whl, w gear. It stopt. Wrk esr aftr, all of us, nobdy can say why. Bran 20 & 6. Hv cut this so nxt one can fnd it.*
> Sila Carver, first hand, the ninth day, the first line cut into the board

**Hand:** Sila Carver
**Day:** 9
**Place:** the Memorial ground, Ashfall Hold

You cannot dig a grave in ash ground the way you dig one anywhere else, and nobody in Ashfall Hold knew that at first light on the ninth day and all of them knew it by the middle of the morning.

Sila Carver cut the turf off first, in strips, with the hay knife, and rolled the strips and stood them on end at the head of the ground so they would keep. Under the turf there is a thin mat of root, and under the mat there is a hand's depth of grey, and under the grey there is the old ground the Mourne buried. The grey cuts like flour. The spade goes into it without a sound and comes out full and there is nothing to lean on. She had it down to a knee's depth in a quarter of an hour and it looked like a grave, and then the long side facing the fields ran.

It ran the way meal runs. The whole face of it came in at once and filled the bottom back up to her ankles, and she stood in it and looked at it.

What she did about it is why the Memorial ground at Ashfall Hold is shaped the way it is. She cut it wider than a grave needs at the top, four foot by seven, and put a step in the long sides a hand below the turf line, and she sent the Dunns down the slope for two hurdles and stood them against the ends and drove stakes outside them at a slant to hold them off. Then she dug inside the hurdles. The grey still ran but it ran onto the step and stopped there, and you can see the step now if you look at the low side of that ground in dry weather, and there is a step cut into the long sides of every grave in this valley, and every Carver since has been taught to cut it, and not one of them in two hundred days has been told why.

The two spoils came out in two heaps and did not mix. The grey went to the north and the old dark ground went to the south, because Sila would not have them shovelled back in wrong order into the same hole. They are still two heaps. Grass took the southern one in a month. Nothing has ever grown on the northern one.

Four of them dug. Sila Carver, Asha Vane, Ketil Dunn and Orrin Dunn. It took from first light to about noon. Bran Holt cut four crosses on the Carvers' stick and four on the Field-hands', because he would not put labour to the job it was taken from.

·  ·  ·

The argument about the gear happened in the grave, with Sila standing in it, which is where Ketil Brack came to have it.

The gear was the hook, her belt knife, her boots, and the whetstone cloth off her belt. The hook was good iron. The Hold had four hooks and one Smith and no iron in the valley but a crate of scrap that Ketil Brack had been carrying for nine years, and he wanted the hook out. He said it reasonably and he said it early and he made an offer with it: he would cut a hook out of thorn that morning, shape and weight, and it could go in instead, and nobody would know the difference from four paces.

Sila Carver did not argue about what the dead are owed. She is a Carver. She argued about how things are made.

She pointed at the Hearth door, which is eleven paces off and in sight of that ground, and she said the doorstone. She said the dip in it was not in the stone when it was quarried and it is in the stone now, and the feet that put it there are not in this valley and were never in this valley, and the dip is here and will be here when everybody standing in this hole is not. She said a haft worn to a hand is the same thing done the other way about. She said that if what had been out in the west field for a day and a night was her grip, then her grip had closed around things, and the things were in it, and you cannot put a hand in the ground and leave out what the hand was closed around and call it whole.

Then she said the sentence that is in her wax and is in every copy of this Chronicle in four valleys.

*We buried her with her knife. The knife was part of the grip.*

Ketil Brack stood at the edge of the hole for a while and then went and got the hook off the cart and brought it back and put it in himself. He laid it along her right side with the point down toward the feet, which is how you lay a hook down when you are not coming back to it that day.

He put the hook in with his own hands. On the thirty-first day he stood at his own fire with a boy's tongs in front of him and would not do it again.

·  ·  ·

They went down for her at noon with a hurdle and the fence rope and five people.

What they had learned they had learned off Ketil Brack's back. Nobody went in on the fourth pass. They went in on the second, from the left and from behind, two on the hurdle and two on the rope and one out in front where she could reach and nothing else could be reached. Colm Grey walked in front. He had said leave her the night before and he walked in front, and he is the only man in this Chronicle who ever did the thing he had argued against without being asked to and without saying anything about it after.

They put the hurdle against her back and took her legs out from under her with the rope and she went down onto the hurdle. She was heavy. Ketil Dunn said she was as heavy as the mill stone had been and the mill stone had been in water. Six of them carried her in the end and they changed hands twice in two hundred paces going up the headland.

She did not stop. That is the part to be accurate about. She was on her back on a hurdle with her legs bound at the knee and her waist roped to the frame, being carried up a slope by six people, and the left hand went out and closed on nothing at about knee height and drew nothing in to the hip, and the right arm came across low in the flat arc and hit the frame, and the shoulders turned as far as the rope would let them, and the weight shifted in her hips against the boards. And at the end of every fourth pass the right hand went out to the side and drew along the cloth that was not there, twice, once each way, and the Dunns had to lean away from it at the front corners, and they learned the count going up the hill and after a hundred paces they were leaning without being told.

Rin Ashfall walked beside the hurdle with her hand on her mother's shoulder the whole way and did not take it off when the shoulder turned under it.

Wren Ashfall walked in front of Colm Grey and did not look back once.

They set the hurdle down beside the hole. Sila Carver went in first. Then four of them lifted her off the hurdle and she went in, and she could not be laid flat, because the arm came back, and Sila had known that since the seventh day and had cut the hole for it. She was laid on her right side with the knees drawn up, which is how she had set, and Sila put the hook's haft into the curve of the right hand.

It fitted.

That is in Sila's hand, two words, and they are the only two words in the first twenty days of this Chronicle that I have never been able to read out loud.

The knife went at the belt. The cloth went at the belt, on the right, where she had been reaching for it. The boots stayed on her feet.

Then they filled it in. Wren Ashfall would not put a spade in and nobody asked her to. Rin Ashfall put the first of it in with her hands, which was the grey off the northern heap, and after that they used the spades and worked from both ends.

It stopped in the middle of a pass.

The right arm was coming across in the flat arc, at shin height, and it came to the end of the arc and the shoulder let go and the arm lay where it had arrived. The left hand was closed and it opened. And then it was an arm and a hand, and the earth went on going in on top of them, and the Hold had been hearing that half step for a day and a night and four hours and none of them noticed the quiet for a moment because there was the sound of spades.

Sila Carver's hand says it stopped when the earth was at the wrist.

Sable Kettle said, to me, at that Hearth, a hundred and forty days later, that it stopped before there was any earth on her at all, and that it stopped when the hook went into the hand, and that she was standing at the head of the hole and saw it, and that Sila Carver was in the hole at the time with her back to it.

Both of them were there. One of them was in the hole. I record what was said. I do not settle it.

·  ·  ·

Then there was the thing that happened to all of them, and the fourteen accounts of it are the closest this Chronicle ever comes to fourteen people saying the same thing.

Orrin Dunn was on a spade and stopped, and looked at the heap, and looked at what he had moved off it, and could not account for the two. He said he was not out of breath. He said he had been on that spade for the length of it and he should have been and he was not.

Wren Ashfall held her own hands out in front of her and turned them over. They had been shaking since the sixth day. She had strapped Ketil Brack's ribs with them shaking and she had told nobody. They were not shaking.

Yeva Holt went down for water and came back up the rise with two full buckets and arrived at the top with them still in her hands, and stood there holding them, and then set them down and went back for two more that nobody needed.

Ketil Brack went to the Smithy in the afternoon, because he had a coulter on the floor and had made up his mind that morning what he was going to do with it, and the fire came up faster than it had any business coming up. He checked the charcoal. He checked it again. He put his arms into the flue to the elbow to see whether something had shifted in the throat of it, which is a thing he had built himself and would not have doubted on any other day. Then he stopped checking and worked, and he worked that fire until dark and got more out of it than he had got out of it on the seventh day with the same fuel, and he did not tell anybody for two days.

Rin Ashfall took the long spade off Sila and finished the filling and levelled it and then did not put the spade down for an hour.

Bran Holt's word for it is the one the Hold kept. He said that something that had been leaning on them had stopped leaning.

Colm Grey said nothing and walked the fence twice.

Nobody said it was good. Two people said out loud that it was wrong, and one of the two was Wren Ashfall, who had buried her sister at noon, and who said, at the fire, in front of everybody, that she did not want to be paid for it. Nobody answered her. Bran Holt wrote the sentence down eleven days later out of memory and put it in the middle of a line about hurdles, where I found it, and I have taken it out of the middle of that line and set it here where it goes.

The Hold got more work out of the afternoon of the ninth day than out of any afternoon before it. That is a fact and it is on the sticks. They finished the last fence at Wrenfield, all eleven stakes, and they got the skin off the lye vat and the vat drawn off clear into two of the hides, and they cut and stood sods for the Smithy's other half, and Bran Holt had written in the morning what he expected the day to give and he wrote at dusk what it had given and the second number was six over the first.

·  ·  ·

Bran Holt had been counting since the second day and he did the sum that evening sitting on the boards by the fire with six sticks in his lap.

He counted what it had cost. From the sixth day to the ninth he had crosses for the watch at the cart, and the argument, and the two mornings nobody would go near the west field, and Ketil Brack's two ribs, and four hands digging from first light to noon, and the six who carried her up the headland, and the afternoon he had lost on the seventh day getting nothing done at all. He added the crosses and did not add the notches, because what he was after was the hole and not the year. It came to twenty half-days of work the Hold had not had.

Then he wrote the six.

He wrote them both on the board in charcoal under the rota, twenty and six, one above the other, and he did not write what they were of.

I have his sticks. His twenty was half-days of labour. His six was hurdles, or it was the difference between what he had expected and what came in, which on the ninth day was the same six. That is all it was. That is the whole of it.

The Vale says twenty for a Burial now. It says it at Successions and it says it on the board and it says it when it is short and cannot afford one, and the Tellers say six for what a Burial gives, and every hold in four valleys that has a copy of this Chronicle says twenty and six, and none of them mean half-days and hurdles, and Bran Holt did. No hand since has been able to say what the numbers are of. Every hand since has used them. I use them. I have used them in this book eleven times before this page and I did not stop to say so.

Nine graves later, every person who has carried the blade in this valley has carried what the Hold got out of Oda Ashfall's grave on the ninth day. It goes to all of them. It cannot be given back and it cannot be lost. Ten people have been stronger because fourteen people buried a woman who fell on a stake, and the stronger they got the more of them there were to bury. I have written that sentence out four times and it does not improve.

·  ·  ·

Sila Carver cut the first line into the board that night, and then she cut the eight days before it.

She did the nine days in one sitting out of the charcoal rota and out of her own memory, back to the first day, which is why the beginning of this Chronicle is a reconstruction made in the dark by a tired woman with a finite stock of wax, and why the fourth day has no weather in it, and why the third day's argument about the Wardens is four words long. Everything anybody knows about the founding of Ashfall Hold, this book included, comes through one evening's work by somebody who had been digging since before light.

Her reason is in her hand and it is the last part of the line I have set at the head of this chapter.

She wrote it down so the next one could find it.

That is the whole of the founding of the Chronicle and it is not a noble sentiment. It is a Carver's habit. A Carver writes down what is on site, and what materials went in, and what the sizes were, so that whoever comes to mend it is not starting from nothing. She had found out on the ninth day that there was a thing you could do, and what it cost, and that it worked, and she wrote the reason next to the result the way you write the length of a joist next to the span, and she wrote it where the next person to stand at the edge of a hole would look.

*The next one.* Nine days into the Hold, with fourteen people alive in it and one in the ground, she had already assumed there would be a next one, and she was right, and there were nine.

Rin Ashfall asked, at the fire, whether that meant they should have done it on the sixth day.

Nobody answered her. Bran Holt got up and went and got his sticks and counted the three days again, which is an answer and is the only one on record.

·  ·  ·

Sila Carver set two posts on that ground before she slept, with the plumb line, one at the head of the grave and one at the foot, and she got them true, and the hoarding went round it later out of the bed of the cart. She never used that plumb line for anything else. She had a lead bob on waxed cord and it was for the Memorial posts and for nothing else, and the Carvers of this Hold have kept that rule since without anybody having made it a rule.

The first hand does not use the word Memorial. In Sila's wax that ground is one mark, the mark she used for the site, with a stroke under it. The word arrived later and I do not know who brought it. Bran Holt's hand has it from the twentieth day as though it had always been there.

Then she went and got the pane.

It was under the bench where Oda Ashfall had put it back on the fifth day, in its fleece, a single sheet, a hand and a half square, greenish at the edge where it had thickened, with a bloom on one face that would not come off with spit and cloth. It had come out of a house that was not hers, in a valley she did not talk about, and she had carried it across two valleys on a cart with a cracked hub and had not let anybody else touch it, and she had never told one person in this Hold what it was for.

Ketil Brack asked where the next one came from.

He asked it that night and he asked it as a Smith, and his answer to his own question was that he did not have one. He said he could get sand to a heat. He said he could not get it to run, and that there was something wanted in it that he did not have and could not name, and that when he found out what it was he would say so. It took him sixty-three days. In the meantime this ground took names it could not put in glass, and went on taking them faster than glass could be made, and what that did to the order of the panes is a later page and not this one.

Sila Carver cut ODA ASHFALL into the glass with a graver by firelight, in the top corner, small.

She left the rest of the pane empty.

Nobody asked her why. She set it against the head post on the Memorial ground with the cut face in, out of the weather, and it has been in that ground and then in the frames after that ground had frames, and it is the oldest pane in this valley and the only one with room on it.

There is a cairn four paces from Oda Ashfall's grave. It was on that rise before the Hold was, and it has round stones on it that were carried up from the lake, and Sable Kettle knew whose it was before she reached it on the first day, and Sila Carver wrote it down on the fourth day under materials because a Carver writes down what is on site.

Bran Holt's hand calls Oda Ashfall's the oldest grave in the Vale. He wrote that on the twentieth day and he was standing four paces from the cairn when he wrote it. There has never been a line on that board about the child under it. There has never been a verb for her. I have gone through all eighteen and there is not one that fits, and I have looked at this for seventy days, and the Hold that invented the Keeping on its ninth day founded its Memorial on a grave it had not dug and has never once counted.

I record what was said. I do not settle it.
