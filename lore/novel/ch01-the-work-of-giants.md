# Chapter 1: The Work of Giants

> *Work of giants. Nobody now living could raise it, and nobody now living knows why they would.*
>
> what the Wayfarers say at any wall, and say first on the Vane Road

**Hand:** Grieve Mourne, compiling · **Days:** the two hundred and tenth, and then the first · **Place:** the Vane Road, then the Vale

I went out on the evening of the two hundred and tenth day with the Hold still drinking behind me.

There is no verb for going out to look at a wall. I have gone looking for one for seventy days. There are eighteen and none of them is that, so the walk is not in the Chronicle. It is only here, in this, which is a different kind of thing, and I will come to what kind.

The Wake was held that afternoon. What a Wake is, and what it cost, and what it bought, is set down in its own place near the back of this book with the four numbers it moved, and I am not going to spend it here. I say only that the whole Hold drinks, that everything unspent is spent at once, and that when it is finished the Hearth is very quiet and nobody in it has anything left over to be careful with. I could not sit in that. I took the blade, and a graver, and a wax board, and I went south past the last fence and down the Vane Road.

The road is older than we are. It is worn in the middle the way a road is worn by feet and not by wheels, a shallow trough a foot across that holds water after rain and holds it in the same places every time. Somebody walked it for a long while. Fall-ash lies along both verges where the wind has put it, grey, with grass coming up through it, and where the verge has slipped you can see the old surface under the ash, which is packed stone, laid on purpose, graded to throw water off to the west.

Then the ground narrows between two shoulders of rock, and the wall stands across it.

It is eleven courses to the top where the top is still there. The stones came off Stonewake. I know that because I have carried Stonewake stone, and it breaks with a flat grey face and a rust-coloured seam through it, and every stone in that wall has the seam running the same way, which means somebody stood at the quarry and turned each one before it was loaded. The courses are dressed. The joints are close enough that you cannot start a knife in them, and they were not laid dry: there is lime in the beds, white lime, burnt hard, still gripping. In three places along the top the facing is gone and you can see into the core, and the core is rubble with lime poured over it rather than packed in, which is what a builder does when there is more lime than patience. To have more lime than patience you need kilns drawn all summer, and men to draw them, and wood cut and carted to burn under them, and somebody sitting down at the end of every week counting what came out.

Where is the one who counted it. Where is the hand that dressed the third course and cut a bird's foot into the face of every stone it set, three strokes, and cut it nowhere else in this valley. Where is the water that was carried up to them in the heat, and the path it came up, which is still here, worn in the middle, going nowhere now but to a shut gate. Where are the carts, and the oxen, and the rope, and the scaffold that stood against the west face long enough to leave four square sockets in the second course that nobody has ever filled in. Where has it gone.

At the crossing below the wall there is a post, and on the post there are six characters cut in a line, which is a Wayfarer's mark and means that there is a hold this way with a board on it that you can write on. Somebody has scored a single deep line through all six. The cut of the score is not the cut of the characters: it is shallower at the start and it runs out at the end, which is what a knife does when the hand using it is in a hurry. I have asked three Wayfarers who scored it and got two answers and a refusal, and the two answers were not the same answer, and neither of the people named was in a position to have done it. I record what was said.

It is not that the craft is lost. Lime is lime. We draw kilns at Stonewake every summer and I could tell you the stone and the mix and the days. Work of giants is what the Wayfarers say, and they do not mean tall men. They mean a number of people that does not exist any more. There is nobody to quarry it. There is nobody to want it. The wall was not built by better hands than ours. It was built by more of them.

The gate is in the wall and the gate is shut. It is shut with two arms' worth of squared timber dropped into sockets cut in the stone of the inside face. I have stood at the joint with my eye to it and seen the timber, and the sockets, and the dust lying on top of the timber.

Asha Vane's hand says that on the ninety-ninth day they took that timber down, went in, came out, and put it back. The Wayfarer copy that reached us out of Holt Vale says the gate was barred when the Ashfall people arrived and barred when they left, and that they went over the wall on a ladder in two places. Asha Vane was there. The Holt Vale copy was cut by a woman who was not there, from something she was told in another valley in another season, and she cut it in a good clear hand and put her mark on it. I did not lift the timber to see which side the dust lay thicker on. I record what was said. I do not settle it.

The board is inside the gate and it is legible. Vane Hold's board, planed, four numbers cut into it rather than written on it, and one line under the four numbers, which is the line that is at the bottom of every board in every valley. That line is legible too. I have both of them. They are copied out exactly in Asha Vane's chapter, where they belong, because she read them first and because she is the one with the right to be angry about them. I am not going to put them here for the shape of it.

I sat with my back to the timber until the light went out of the road. Nothing came down it. I do not believe anything had for a long time.

· · ·

I am the fourth hand.

Sila Carver kept the first, from the founding to the twentieth day. Her letters are cramped and she cut them short to save wax, so that a word like *weather* comes out as three strokes and a hook, and you learn her the way you learn a dialect. She recorded the weather every day without exception and she recorded it with suspicion. *Fair and I do nt trust it.* She wrote the first page eight days after the day it describes and dated it back to the first, and I have let the date stand where she put it.

Bran Holt kept the second, from the twentieth day to the ninety-sixth. He is legible. He is complete. He is very dull, and he recorded nothing that he was not asked to record, and I have held that against him for seventy days. Every time I have gone into the Chronicle looking for something that is not there, it is not there because Bran Holt was not asked. He wrote down four numbers and one sentence and the day, fifty-nine times fewer than I would have liked, and he never once wrote down what a room felt like.

Asha Vane kept the third, from the ninety-sixth day to the hundred and fortieth. She is fast and she is angry. She argues with the entries she is copying, in the margin, in the same hand as the entries, so that you cannot always tell which is the record and which is the quarrel with it. She was pulled out of the Carvers' shed on the ninety-fifth day and put into a Warden's kit, and she kept the board on her knee inside the kit, which is why some of her days are cut on the flat of a shield.

I have kept it since the hundred and fortieth.

Besides the three hands I have villager testimony, taken from whoever was standing there, which is worth what standing there is worth. I have the panes at the Memorial. I have the board on the Vane Road. And I have four Wayfarer copies out of four other valleys, none of which agree with one another about us, and one of which has us ending twice.

The panes carry a name and a day and nothing else, which is why people say nobody disputes them. People are wrong about that. The name is not disputed. The day sometimes is. There is a pane in the west range with a day on it that is one off from the day on the board, and the woman who cut the pane was standing in the field when it happened and the man who cut the entry was not, and neither of them is alive to be asked, and the pane and the board are eighteen paces apart and have sat there disagreeing for most of a year. I have copied both. I record what was said. I do not settle it. I will say once, here, that I have come to prefer the panes, and that preferring a source is not the same as settling anything, and that I know exactly how thin that distinction is.

The rule is the one the Tellers have always said and the one I said out loud at the Hearth before I cut a line: I record what was said. I do not settle it.

This is not modesty. A ledger is kept by adding to the bottom of it, and nothing in it is ever crossed out, which is the only reason the arguments are still in it. Maren Verren told Rin Ashfall's death aloud in front of everyone on the twenty-first day and got two details wrong, and two people corrected her, and Bran Holt wrote down the telling and both corrections and did not choose. That is the best thing Bran Holt ever did and he did it because he was not asked to choose. Where the copies differ, I have copied the difference. Where two hands give two accounts of one afternoon, both accounts stay, and if that is unsatisfying then so was the afternoon.

The shape of this book follows from that, and I am not going to apologise for it. It is a ledger and not a story. There are two hundred and ten days in it and fifty-nine entries, so most days have nothing, and the ones that have something have it in a fixed form: the day, the verb, the subject, the four numbers moved, and one sentence. There are eighteen verbs. There have only ever been eighteen, and I did not choose them either.

It also runs out of order, because the roads ran out of order. A man who wants to know what happens when you leave a body lying in a field does not want to be told about it in four places sixty days apart with other people's weddings in between. So the middle of this book is grouped by road: the unburied, the water, the sick, the shaved names, the strangers, the stone. The lineage is separate and comes after, because the lineage only ever goes one way. Where a chapter sits out of its year I say so in a line and move on.

· · ·

The Vale is ninety-six by seventy-two. Wayfarers write it that way on the back of the folded page, and so do we, and nobody I have asked can tell me what the numbers count. Paces will not do it. Neither will days. I have asked four Wayfarers and been given three answers and a shrug.

What it means in the body is this: one place at a time. As much ground as one person can cross in a day and get back off before dark. You do not go to two places. You go to the mere or you go to the scarp, and whichever one you go to is the one the day is spent on.

At the north head there is the Mourne. It is a fire-mountain and it is dead. It has one shoulder fallen away so that the notch faces us, and on that side the snow will not lie. Its last breath laid a hand's depth of grey ash over everything in this valley, once, before any of this, and that ash is the reason anybody ever came here. What the ash does is the next chapter and I will not spoil the work of it.

Under the Mourne is the Grey Ash. Fall-ash and drawn-ash mixed together past the telling apart, ankle deep where the wind banks it, trees standing dead upright in it because nothing comes to bring them down. It is quiet in a way that you notice with your body before you notice it with your ears. People walk north into it on purpose. That is a road and it has its own chapter.

East of the Hold the ground goes up into Stonewake Scarp: the quarry, the lime-kilns below it with the white dust on everything within a hundred feet, and above the quarry the barrows. There are things standing in the barrows in packed plate that have been standing a long time. Two of our wielders are in the ground because of what is up there and one of them is in the ground twice.

South and east of the scarp is Kettlemere, which is a lake, and under the lake is Low Kettle. It was a village. The water came up before we came here and it is still up, and their dead are down there at a depth that no Teller can go to and come back from, which has consequences for this book that I will state plainly when I get to them and again at the end.

South of that is Brackwood, which is trees: charcoal hearths, the burners' camps, and the quarantine huts, which were put up in a hurry on the forty-first day and are still standing.

West of the Hold is Wrenfield. Fields first, then the last fence, and then the long grass. The grass is taller than a person and it is where the Hold eats from and it is where things come out of, and both of those are true at once and always were.

And the Hold itself, which sits on a low rise above the fields with water on two sides of it, because Oda Ashfall stood on that rise on the first day and said that she wanted to see the grass move. There was already one grave on the rise when they came. It was a child's, out of Low Kettle, carried up and laid there by her own people before the water closed over their village, and it was the first thing the Hold had and nobody put it there. Sila Carver wrote it down on the fourth day under materials, because she did not know where else to put it.

The rest of it is the Hearth, which is the only safe fire and the only board; the Memorial, which is where the panes are and where a Vigil is held; the Smithy, hot end and cold end; the fields and the workshop; and the palisade, which is what stands between the Hold and whatever has been left standing out there too long. The palisade has been tested once in two hundred and ten days. That is one more time than anybody wanted and one fewer than we deserved.

· · ·

I came back up the Vane Road in the dark and did not go into the Hearth. I sat outside it on the step, where the stone has a dip in it from people coming through, and I put the wax board on my knee.

The Chronicle begins on a day nobody was writing. There was no board on the first day and no reason for one. Sila Carver was a Carver, and a Carver counts, so she had wax with her for tallying boards and nails and sods and hours, and on the first day she tallied the day itself out of habit, in four strokes, on the edge of a materials board, because that is what her hands did with an evening.

Eight days later she had a reason, and she went back to that board and cut the first page of this out of the tally that was already on it. So the first page of the Chronicle of Ashfall Hold was written eight days late, by a woman who had spent those eight days finding out what the eight days were for, and she dated it to the first anyway. I have thought about correcting the date. I am not going to. She was there and I was not.

Here it is, whole, letters as she cut them.
