# Chapter 43: The Wake

> *Every name was spoken, every Echo laid down. The settlement outlives its dead and
> remembers them without fear.*
> Recorded: the board, day two hundred and ten, locked

**Hand:** Grieve Mourne, fourth hand, writing about himself, which no Teller has done
**Days:** 204 to 210
**Place:** the Grey Ash; the Memorial; the Hearth; and last of all the shallows at
Kettlemere

I am going to write this chapter in the first person, once, and then never again.

I have spent seventy days compiling four hands into one book and keeping my own out of it,
which is the discipline, and which I have broken exactly here because there is no honest
way to record a Succession you are the subject of in the third person. Bran Holt would
have found a way. Bran Holt found a way for everything and the cost was that his Chronicle
tells you nothing.

So: I took the blade on the two hundred and ninth day, and I had never carried anything,
and I am the only wielder of Ashfall Hold who did not fall.

·  ·  ·

Wren Ashfall went north on the two hundred and fourth day and nobody asked her to.

The Grey Ash had been at Ashen since the turn of the season and was still going. That is
drawn-ash over the soil and trees standing dead upright in it, and the last state past
that one has no growth in it at all, no sound, ash to the ankle, and the routes closed. The
Hold had never cleared any of it. In two hundred and three days the Hold had cleared
Brackwood once and Wrenfield twice and had never once put a spade into the ground under
the Mourne, because the Mourne is where the dead go when they are not brought home, and by
the two hundredth day there had been a great many of them.

Her argument, and she made it at the Hearth on the two hundred and third with the board
behind her, was that every Echo the Vale had lost had gone north, and that the reason the
north was Ashen was that it had been eating what the Hold failed to fetch for two hundred
days, and that this was therefore not a piece of geography, it was a debt.

Nobody contradicted her. Orrin Dunn had been dead seven days and she had reclaimed him
herself on the two hundred and third, and the board had said The Wake that evening for
the eleventh time in a hundred and fifty-seven days, and she had stood in front of it and
read the line out loud twice.

She was the first person in the Hold's history to look at that line and treat it as an
instruction.

The board has said The Wake eleven times. It said it on the fifty-third day because a
woman walked past a Gravewarden. It said it on the ninety-first and the hundred and
sixteenth and the hundred and thirty-fifth and the hundred and forty-seventh and the
hundred and sixty-eighth and the two hundred and third, every time for the same reason,
which is that somebody had gone out and fetched somebody, and it stopped saying it every
time for the same reason, which is that somebody else had died and was lying out. Eleven
times. It is not a prophecy. It is a housekeeping report.

Wren Ashfall was the second widow this book has, one hundred and eighty-two days after
the first, and she had the Ashblade, which by then had been carried by four wielders and
had the coulter's curve in it still, and she went north with three flasks and a spade.

·  ·  ·

She had six days. I can give the days because I was cutting the board that week and the
board has them.

On the two hundred and fifth she cleared and set stone at the first of the old rings, and
there were nine rings in the near Grey Ash that the Foragers had mapped and never gone
into, and nobody in the Hold knew whose they were, because whoever had failed to fetch
them had failed before Bran Holt started counting days-lying-out and there was no entry.

On the two hundred and sixth she came back for lime and did not stay the night.

On the two hundred and seventh and eighth she was out, and the two Foragers who went with
her as far as the ash line said she was working at the rate of a person who had done the
arithmetic on how long she had and had not liked the answer.

On the two hundred and ninth she met a Gravewarden at the ninth ring.

·  ·  ·

There is one witness and it is a Forager, and I have his account and it is short, because
he was a hundred paces off with a barrow of shell and no weapon.

Ninety of what a body has. Eighteen of damage, which is twice what a Husk does and half
again what a Stalker does. Fifty-five of packing, which is the number that matters, and
which means that until the plate is broken you are hitting a wall with a purpose. Thirty
of reach on a thing that moves at thirty, so it cannot chase you and does not need to.

And six tenths of a second of tell.

That is the longest warning anything in the Vale gives you. You can watch it arrive. You
can count while it arrives. Every Warden the Hold has ever trained has been told that a
Gravewarden is the most honest thing in the valley and that people die to it anyway,
because six tenths of a second is long enough to decide twice and the second decision is
usually the wrong one.

The Forager says she broke the packing. He says he heard it go, which is a sound like a
seam giving in wet timber, and that she was in on it, and that she had it, and that the
ash under her was drawn-ash at the ninth ring and had been drawn for longer than the Hold
had existed.

She went down to one knee. Not from a blow. From the ash.

I have written that sentence before in this book, on the fourteenth day, about a
nineteen-year-old in chest-high grass, and I want it noticed that I have not varied it.

The Forager says she looked up the slope, which from the ninth ring runs to the shoulder
of the Mourne, and that there is nothing on that slope, no grass, no colour, nine rings and
a dead orchard and ash to the ankle.

He says she said the hillside was worth looking at.

I have had eleven accounts of nine deaths and seven of them contain that line and four of
them are certainly invention. Sable Kettle said it in Wrenfield when Wrenfield was Gone.
Wren Ashfall said it at the ninth ring on ground with nothing on it. I do not know whether
by the two hundred and ninth day it was courage or quotation and I have said elsewhere in
this book that I do not think they knew either, and I will add here that I no longer think
the distinction is worth as much as I used to think it was.

Then she died, and that is the flat fact, and it wants no help from me.

·  ·  ·

The Succession was held that evening and there was almost nobody to call.

I want the roll read out properly because the size of it is the whole story of the two
hundred and ninth day. Ashfall Hold on that evening had thirty-one people. Nine of them
were children. Four were the surviving sick out of Brackwood, who were a hundred and
sixty-two days past the quarantine and could not hold a cup for a full minute. Two were
Foragers in the field. Eleven were in the fields and the workshop and were the whole of the
Hold's output, and the Hold was carrying enough grief that its output was worth about four
parts in five of eleven people, and everybody in the room could do that sum.

That leaves five. Two Healers, who by standing law of the Hold cannot be called, a law
made on the twenty-fourth day and never once suspended. A Warden with a shoulder. A
Carver, who was nineteen and whom I had apprenticed myself and would not put forward.

And the Teller.

Three names were called, because three names are always called. Mine was the third and
the other two were declined by the people who held them, which is permitted and has
happened twice before, and I am not going to put their names in this book, because the
book will outlast them and their children.

The words said over me were the words. *Drank with the dead most nights.*

They were true. Wren Ashfall and I drank at the Hearth most nights for eleven years,
because she was in the fields and I was at the board and we were the only two people in the
Hold who were up late for work rather than for company. We were not family. We were not
lovers. There is no word in the Vale for what we were and the nearest one the board has is
Friend, which is worth an eighth more of Resolve than nothing and does not touch the
purse at all.

What it does is make the rites cheap.

A Friend pays four in five. Nobody knows why. It has been observed since the hundred and
forty-first day, when Sable Kettle buried Tarn Stonewake and the board came up short of
what the arithmetic said it should, and Asha Vane wrote three angry paragraphs about it
which amount to *it is cheaper and I cannot tell you why.*

I have thought about this more than any other number in this book. The whole of the two
hundred and tenth day, the thing that the Vale is going to remember Ashfall Hold for,
every name spoken and every Echo laid down and the board locked on the best of the five
lines, all of it was affordable because a Teller and a Field-hand used to sit up late
together.

That is the arithmetic. I have no argument against it and I would like one.

·  ·  ·

I was bad at it. I want that recorded properly, by me, while I can still be trusted to do
it.

I am thirty-two. I have cut glass and wax since I was twelve and I have the forearms for
it and nothing else. I had never held the Ashblade. I had held the Widow's Maul once, off
the wall, in the yard, on a bet, and had put it down again.

I went out on the two hundred and tenth day at dawn, because I am not Maren Verren and
had no reason to avoid the hour, and I took four flasks.

Four, because of a Pyre held on the thirty-first day for a smith's apprentice whose name
was Bel Brack and who died at the fire at seventeen, and because his gear was burned with
him after Maren Verren worked Ketil Brack's fire for two days and let the arithmetic say
itself.

I could take more hits than Rin Ashfall could. Six more, and then six more again, and
again, and again, because the Hold has held Burials and each one of them is worth six to
every successor who comes after, forever, and cannot be lost.

What came back to me when I stopped swinging came back faster than it came back to her, by
eight in a hundred and eight in a hundred again, because the Hold has held Tellings, and a
Telling is a death recounted aloud in full by somebody who saw it, and the first one was
held on the twenty-first day by a widow who got two details wrong.

And the blade hit harder in my hands than it hit in hers, by a tenth and a tenth again,
because there are Monuments standing at Wrenfield and at the scarp and in Brackwood, with
relics under them, raised over Echoes that somebody went out and fetched.

I am not a better man than Rin Ashfall and I am not a better hand with a sword than any of
the eight between us. I am a worse one than all of them. I went out on the two hundred and
tenth day carrying nine people's deaths, properly kept, and it was enough.

This is the sentence this whole book has been walking toward and I have put off writing it
for forty pages: **the lineage is stronger because those people died.** Not braver.
Stronger, measurably, in the arm and the chest and the purse, and the measure is on the
board and anybody can check it.

I do not know what to do with that and neither did any of the three hands before me.

·  ·  ·

Wren Ashfall's ring was four days old and at the second growth and it had not left the
ninth ring, because they do not leave until the third.

I will not write a set piece. There is one in this book already, on the nineteenth day,
taken down from a woman still shaking, and it has not been improved on in two hundred
days and will not be improved on by me.

What I will record is the method, because the method is the only thing in this account
that is any use to whoever comes next.

I did not try to fight it. I read it. Six tenths of a second, and I have spent twenty
years looking at a thing until I am certain what it says, and that is the only skill I
brought out there. I let it come and I moved and I did not swing, and I did that for a
long time, and the purse filled by eight and eight in a hundred while I was not swinging,
and it did not fill at all for the thing in front of me because it is not a person and
does not have one.

It had Orrin Dunn's habit. That is what the second growth gave it, and I knew the habit,
because I had watched Orrin Dunn in the yard for twenty-three days and written down what he
did: he stepped in on the trailing side and came round. She had not had that habit in
life. She had it on the two hundred and tenth day because she had killed the man who did
and reclaimed him and carried his motion out north with her, and now it had them both.

I went in on the fourth exchange, on the trailing side, which is the side Ketil Brack
ground a short edge into on the sixteenth day and never explained.

Then it went down properly. I have taken Sila Carver's four words because there are no
better ones and because she is dead and cannot object.

The ring stood up in the time it takes to draw a breath. There is no grass at the ninth
ring. What stood up was ash, in a ring eleven paces across, and then settled, and the
Forager with the barrow of shell says it was the worst thing he has seen in his life and
he was at the palisade on the hundred and thirty-fourth day.

The Ashblade came back to my hand and I was already holding it, which is a thing no other
wielder has reported, and I do not know what to make of it.

And the grief arrived. All of it at once, four days of a fed ring on ground that had been
drawing for longer than the Hold had existed, and the board went from twenty-eight to
sixty-two on the walk home, and Bran Holt's sentence from the twentieth day was in my
mouth the whole way: *the ones who go and get them can pay for the funerals.*

·  ·  ·

Every Echo the board had ever counted was down. That was the eleventh time and it was the
last.

A Wake takes sixty. I paid forty-eight.

The whole Hold drinks. That is the rite and there is nothing else to it: no words, no
graver, no stone, no fire. Thirty people and a cask in the yard and every name spoken
once, in order, oldest first.

Oda Ashfall, who died of an ordinary thing on the sixth day and walked to the field on the
eighth with nothing in her hands.

Rin Ashfall. Nineteen. Four days.

Bel Brack, who was eleven on the palisade frame and seventeen at the fire.

Maren Verren, who went past the doorstone for sixty-nine days.

Sila Carver, first hand, whose own pane has the S wrong.

Colm Grey, who killed two Gravewardens and a third growth and was killed by a Husk because
he was out of everything.

Yeva Holt, who reached the palisade on the hundred and thirty-fourth day carrying the
Ashblade, and who was fetched at the gate on the hundred and thirty-fifth, and whose Wake
was the first this Hold ever held.

Tarn Stonewake, who never learned her name, and was the most efficient wielder we had, and
whom we liked least, and who is the reason there is anything left to write this in.

Sable Kettle, who carried a kitchen knife for fifteen days and took forty minutes to
reclaim a man on the scarp with it.

Nix Ember, who wanted the blade and said so, and who is buried two ridges east in Holt
glass under a name cut by a woman who was once a Wayfarer at our crossing post and whom
Bran Holt did not think worth a line.

Orrin Dunn, Ketil Dunn's son, who had the eastern furlong off Bran Holt, and who died at
the palisade against an Echo whose name we cannot read.

Wren Ashfall. The last of the founding name in this Hold. Six days at the ninth ring.

Eleven names and one more, said by somebody else, because you cannot say your own at your
own Wake and I was not dead. Sila Verren, a stranger, drifted into the Vale on the
seventieth day out of a valley we have never seen, and buried in our ground, in our glass,
under pearl from her own ash. She is in the west range between Sila Carver and Tarn
Stonewake and she has no bond to anybody here and her pane is cut better than most of
ours.

Everything unspent was spent. The pool went to nothing for the second time in two hundred
and ten days.

And the board locked.

Memory one hundred. Mercy one hundred. Communion one hundred. Dominion one.

·  ·  ·

Dominion one.

Two hundred and ten days. Ten wielders. Nine graves, one pyre, one pane cut for a
stranger, two regions lost and two pushed back, one conscription, one gate opened to four
Wayfarers, one Echo sent out of the valley and buried by people who had never met him.

And at the end of it the Vale came out one step on the side of holding rather than being
held.

Vane Hold reached fifty-one. We reached eleven, once, on the ninety-fifth day, when Colm
Grey pulled a Carver out of her shed on her wedding morning and put her in a Warden's
kit, and she wrote the third hand of this book from inside it, and she was right to be
angry.

I have written the number twice now, in the table and in a sentence, because I do not
believe it either.

·  ·  ·

I walked to Kettlemere on the evening of the two hundred and tenth day, in the dark, with
the yard still going behind me.

The shallows at the north end are where Bel Kettle stood from before the first day of the
Hold until the thirty-eighth, when Maren Verren went in after her with a post-driver that
was the wrong tool and brought back a knife and a shoe.

The water was flat. It is always flat. Low Kettle is under it, and Low Kettle was a
village, and it went down before anybody in Ashfall Hold was born.

There is no rite for water. The Hold has attempted two and both failed and I have written
them up in the appendix with the failures intact.

Bel Kettle was the only one the board ever counted, because she was carried up and laid at
the Hold before the water closed, and a Teller could see her, and a Teller could enter her.

The rest of them are down there in the Holding. They have been in the Holding for longer
than this Chronicle has existed. Nobody has ever surveyed them. Nobody could. What a board
knows is what a hand entered into it, and no hand has ever entered Low Kettle, and so on
the evening of the two hundred and tenth day the board was able to say, truthfully, by its
own arithmetic, that every Echo in the Vale was laid down.

I stood in the shallows to the ankle and I did the only thing I know how to do, which is to
write it down.

Ashfall Hold declared itself finished over a village still in the Holding.

I am not going to settle it. I have not settled anything in four hundred pages and I am not
going to start on the last one. Two things are true: every name this board knows was spoken
on the two hundred and tenth day, and this board does not know everything. Both of those
go in.

The line on the board reads The Wake. It is locked and I locked it. It is also, and I
record this in the same hand and the same ink, a housekeeping report.
