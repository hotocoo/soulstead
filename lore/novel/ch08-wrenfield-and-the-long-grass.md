# Chapter 8: Wrenfield and the Long Grass

> *A minute out is an hour of the Vale's going. We have known it since the third day and we had stopped saying it. On the twelfth day it stopped being a thing we said and became a thing one of us had gone and done.*
> Sila Carver, first hand, day 12

**Hand:** Sila Carver
**Days:** 12
**Place:** Wrenfield

The errand was the fence.

Wrenfield is the near ground and the near ground is where the Hold eats from, and past the last fence the grass goes up past a person's chest and keeps going. Sable Kettle had come back on the eleventh day saying the far fence was down in three places and that she had not gone past it to look. She was asked why not and she did not have an answer that stood up at the Hearth, and she did not change it either. She was fourteen years old and she was the Hold's Forager and she had been right about the ground twice already, and the Hold had not yet worked out that when Sable Kettle would not go on it was worth asking what she had not gone on account of.

So on the twelfth day the Hold sent the one person it had taken off the field column, because that is what it had taken her off it for.

Bran Holt gave her the errand off the rota in the same voice he gave out the bread. Walk the far fence. Count the gaps. Come back. He said the last part twice and then wrote down that he had said it twice.

There was an argument about whether somebody should go with her and it went the way all of those went afterwards, which is to say the rota won it. Fourteen people and six jobs and the west furlong to get in. Colm Grey offered. Ketil Brack said the Smithy could stand a day. Bran Holt read out what a day of the Smithy standing cost in barrow-iron and sickle work, and then read out what a day of Colm Grey off the fence cost, and then read out the bread.

Ketil Brack said the numbers were not the argument.

Bran Holt said he had not said they were, and that he had been asked what the numbers were, and that he had said them.

Nobody went with her. That was the Hold's practice for two hundred days after and nobody ever decided on it. One body outside the palisade at a time, one piece of ground at a time, as much of the Vale as one pair of legs can cross in a day and still get back in. It was arrived at on the twelfth day by a rota being read out loud and it was never once put to the Hearth again.

·  ·  ·

She carried three flasks of grey water at her belt because Wren Ashfall gave her three. Three was not the ration yet. It became the ration on the nineteenth day and the settling of that is a worse story than this one. On the twelfth day it was a number a Healer arrived at standing in front of a shelf with her hand on it, and Wren Ashfall would not say what she had steeped to make them, and nobody in the Hold had yet been burned.

Wren Ashfall was the Hold's Healer, which was the job nobody had wanted, and she was Oda Ashfall's younger sister, which made her the only blood Rin Ashfall had left in the Vale. She said the words a Healer says, which are mostly about pressure. Then she said walk and do not run, and was told that was not a thing that could be arranged.

Rin Ashfall did not drink from the flasks. She had been told they burned going down and she had been told nothing else about them.

She put two fingers in the dip on the doorstone on her way out. Ketil Brack was at the gate. He put his hand up.

·  ·  ·

She went past the last fence a little before the middle of the morning.

Sila Carver's hand for that day has the measurement in it and nothing about what the measurement means. She was gone the part of an hour by her own counting and the Vale spent a day on it. That is the arrangement. Nobody made it and nobody has found the edge of it. The clock runs when there is a body outside the palisade and it does not run when there is not, and inside the Hold nothing moves at all.

The first way Rin Ashfall met that fact was the shadow.

She stopped fifty paces out to look at the first gap in the far fence and the shadow of the Mourne came across Wrenfield at her while she stood still. She saw it come. It crossed the pale barley and then the good barley and then the strip they had not got in yet, at about the pace of a person walking, and it went over her boots and up her and past her and on towards the Hold, and the light on the scarp behind it went from morning to the flat yellow of late in the day in the time it took her to count the gap and decide the gap was two posts wide and not one.

She turned round.

The Hearth smoke was standing in the air above the palisade like a post. Not drifting. Not thinning. Standing, in the shape it had been in when she walked under it, with the same crook in it at the top.

Ketil Brack was in the gate with his hand up.

He had not put it down.

·  ·  ·

She walked the fence.

The gaps were three, as Sable Kettle had said, and one of them was not a gap but a place where something heavy had gone over the top rail and taken the rail with it and put it down flat on the other side, which is a different thing, and she wrote nothing down because she could not write. She counted on her fingers and kept the count.

Between the second gap and the third she went past the place where her mother had stopped on the sixth day.

The grass there is pale at the root. Bran Holt had four stakes in the ground around it, put in on the ninth day at the four quarters, because Bran Holt puts stakes in things. Whether the pale had moved out past the stakes in the three days since is a thing two people said two different ways at the Hearth that night, and both of those are in the Chronicle, and the Chronicle does not say which of them had gone and looked.

Rin Ashfall walked past it on the fence side and her right arm was heavier there than it had been forty paces before, and heavier than it was forty paces after, and she did not say so to anybody that night or afterwards.

The blade was on a hay rope over her shoulder. She took it off the rope going past and carried it in her hand for the rest of the walk and did not put it back.

·  ·  ·

The tall grass starts at the far fence and it does not stop for a mile and a half. It is the same grass. That is the part the Vale has never got used to and has never had any choice about. The Hold eats out of the near end of Wrenfield and the things that come at the Hold come out of the far end of Wrenfield, and between the two ends there is no line, no wall, and no difference in the grass.

·  ·  ·

Here is what comes out of it, set down late, out of place, by a hand that has had two hundred days to get it right and has still only got this far.

They are animals. They were animals of this valley and they are still animals of this valley. They are not anybody. Nothing about them is anybody and that is the only easy thing there is to say about the whole business, and the Hold said it to itself for a long time the way you hold a warm stone.

They are thin. They come up no higher than a man's belt and most of them are shorter than that, and they are long, and there is nothing on them. That is the part that gets said wrong. People who have not been out say they are burnt. They are not burnt. They are worn, the way a handle is worn, down to the shape the work needs and no further, and what has done the wearing is the grey ground they have been living on. A Forager will tell you they have been eating out of drawn-ash for longer than the Hold has existed and that whatever is not in that ground any more is not in them either. What that leaves is quick.

Nobody has ever agreed what they were before. Sila Carver wrote dog. Sable Kettle said deer and would not be argued with, and dressed one out on the thirtieth day to prove it, and proved something, but not that. A Wayfarer's copy from two valleys east has a line in it that says they were hares. Everybody in the Hold laughed at that. Nobody laughed at it twice.

The Chronicle keeps all four and settles none of them.

Sixty-four of speed. Nothing else in the Vale comes near it, and there is only one number in the whole of this business that matters more, and it is twenty-six.

·  ·  ·

They came out at the third gap.

·  ·  ·

The first one she saw and the second one she did not, and that is the whole of what happened in the order it happened.

It came out of the grass on her left at a speed nothing in the Vale had gone at in front of anybody yet. Not a charge. A crossing. It put the ground behind it the way a stone goes across ice. A Forager who saw one four days later said it was like being in the way of something thrown, and that is the best that anybody has ever done with it.

It came in to twenty and stopped, which is its reach, and it made the sound they make, and it gathered.

Twenty-six hundredths. That is the whole of the warning you get. Ketil Brack put a bob on it that night off her telling of it and said twenty-six and has never changed the number. A quarter of a count and a hundredth over. It is enough. That is the worst thing about it. It is enough if you are already standing right and it is nothing at all if you are not.

She was standing right, for once in her life, and for the reason that was going to kill her two days later: she had already decided.

She was twenty-six. It was twenty. She had six on it and six is nothing and six was enough. She swung hard, from the shoulder, the way Ketil had told her eleven times not to, and the arc of a body and a half came round at the height of its neck and the thing came apart at the fence line.

They die to one clean swing. That is the fact and the Vale has it from her.

They do not come alone. The Vale has that from her too, and it cost more.

·  ·  ·

The second one was inside the arc before the iron was up.

She had agreed to the swing. Twenty-eight hundredths of bringing it up, and in the twenty-eight the grass on her right side opened and the second one crossed the ground and came in to twenty and did not stop and did not gather, because it did not need to gather, because she was in it.

There was no halfway. There is no halfway. She had made a promise with both hands eighty hundredths long and the second one arrived in the middle of it.

She kept the promise. The swing went through the first one and out and down and her hands went on out and down with it for the thirty-eight after, because that is what thirty-eight is, and for the whole of the thirty-eight she was a thing standing in a field with its arms in the wrong place.

It opened her on the left side above the hip, the width of two fingers, and went past, and turned in the grass.

Sila's hand, day 12: *Two out of the tall grass at the far fence. She put down one. The other opened her on the left above the hip, two fingers wide, and Wren Ashfall called it twelve. She put down the other one afterwards. Wind from the north all day and no rain.*

Twelve is the first number anybody in the Vale put on being hit and it is the number still.

It is not the blood that matters about twelve and Wren Ashfall knew that before the end of the month. The hit went into her while she had nothing packed on her body to take it, and a hit that lands on a person with nothing on them does not only take the two fingers of flesh. It takes some of the twist out of the rope as well. Rin Ashfall came out of the thirty-eight with her arms back where they belonged and one fewer of the hard ones than the arithmetic said she had, and she was three days from knowing why, and she never did know why, because she had two days.

·  ·  ·

She did the rest of it properly. That should be written down, because most of what was written down about Rin Ashfall afterwards was about the fourteenth day.

She gave up the ground. She went back off the fence line into the short barley where she could see her own feet and she let the second one come to her, and it came, and it came in to twenty and stopped and gathered, and she went sideways and down and over her shoulder and up.

A roll is forty-two hundredths of being a thing that is being carried. You have agreed to that too. For eighteen of the forty-two nothing in the world can reach you, and for the other twenty-four you are on the ground or coming off it and a hand could pick you up. Rin Ashfall did not know either of those numbers. She had the eighteen in her body the way she had always had it, from the hay and the byre roof and out from under the quarry stone on the fourth day, and she had never once had any use for it before the twelfth day.

She used the eighteen four times.

Rolls came cheap to her. Cheap is not free. She was going sideways and down and over and up, and coming up each time with the blade already moving because she could not help it, and each time the light swing went out into where the thing had been twenty-six hundredths ago and not into where it was.

The fifth time she went sideways and down and she did not go over.

What happened instead was a step. A long sideways step, slow, with her weight going out ahead of her and her feet arriving after it, and no part of it fast and no part of it hidden. There is nothing in that step. Nothing in the world stops reaching you in it. It is what the going sideways and down turns into when there is nothing left to spend on it, and it will not save you, and it is not meant as a mercy, and it is not one.

It came through the step and took the shoulder of her coat and the skin under it and she was still standing, and it was inside twenty, and it was in front of her.

She swung light.

Ten hundredths up. Ten in the meat.

Twelve of poise is all a thing that fast can afford to carry. Fourteen is what one light swing of the Ashblade puts into a body. That is the arithmetic of the whole business and it is why they come in twos and threes and fives and why the Hold has never once seen one on its own.

It opened. For about as long as it takes to say a short name it was not a thing crossing ground, it was an animal standing in barley with its weight in the wrong place, and she was already inside the ten in the meat with thirty-four of something to put into it and sixteen of power to put it with.

It went down in the barley and did not get up.

She stood there for a while with the blade down. Sila's hand does not say how long and nobody was there to count it.

·  ·  ·

Then she sat down in the short barley with her back to the fence and tried to get the count of the gaps back and could not.

That is in the Chronicle because she said it out loud at the Hearth that night, in front of everybody, before she said anything at all about the two things in the field. She had been sent out to count the gaps in the far fence. She had counted them. She had held the count in her fingers for the length of the whole fence and then some part of what had happened at the third gap had taken it out of her hand and she could not find it again, sitting in the barley, with both of them down and nothing else coming.

She went back along the fence to look again and got as far as the second gap and turned round, and she was honest about why: the tall grass on the other side of it had not been standing still while she sat down.

She came home with three, which is what Sable Kettle had said on the eleventh day and what she had counted on the way out, and she did not pretend she had counted it twice. Bran Holt put three on the rota board against the errand. Underneath it he put that she had lost the count at the third gap and had it back off a fourteen-year-old Forager, which was not an errand, was not a number, and was nobody's business. Eight days later he had the Chronicle, and he copied his own board into it, including that line, and that is the only reason anybody knows.

·  ·  ·

Coming back she walked in the middle of the field and not along the fence, and she looked at the tall grass the whole way, which is not a useful thing to do and every wielder after her did it too.

She crossed the strip they had not got in yet.

It had been standing when she went out. Six rows of good barley, heavy at the head, two days off cutting, which is a thing you can see about barley at a glance and which everybody in the Hold had been seeing at a glance for a week and being pleased about.

It was flat. The whole strip was over and lying the one way, north to south, from the wind Sila Carver wrote down twice that day and did not think anything of.

Rin Ashfall stood in it and did not understand what she was looking at, and there is no record of anybody explaining it to her, because the two people in the Hold who could have done the sum were doing other sums. The Hold came out on the morning of the thirteenth day and found its strip down and said the word for bad luck and got what it could off the ground with sickles, and set it down as weather.

That is the arrangement. A minute out is an hour of the Vale's going. It is her hour. It is everybody else's barley.

The light went from late afternoon to the last of it while she crossed Wrenfield. The shadow of the Mourne had gone over the Hold and past it and the whole Vale was in it. By the third fence the air had the cold in it that comes off the scarp at the end of a day.

The Hearth smoke was standing above the palisade in the shape it had been in all day, with the same crook in it at the top, and it had not moved, and it did not move while she watched it, and it did not move when she was ten paces out.

Ketil Brack was in the gate with his hand up.

She came through the gate and his hand came down.

She stood on the beaten ground inside the palisade with the blade in her right hand and the blood going down her left leg into her boot, and her hands started, and they went on for some while and she watched them do it, and the eleven people who came out to her watched them do it as well.

Then she looked back out over the palisade at the far end of Wrenfield, where the tall grass was, and she said the grass needed cutting.
