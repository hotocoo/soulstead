# Chapter 7: Rin Ashfall

> *The blade was made on the tenth day and taken on the eleventh. I have put both in one entry because Ketil Brack did not let go of it in between and there is no line to cut between a thing being finished and a thing being handed over.*
> Sila Carver, first hand, day 11

**Hand:** Sila Carver
**Days:** 10 to 11
**Place:** the Smithy, the Hold

The cart had come into the Vale with the Ashfall family and it had not gone out again, there being nowhere to take it. Its wheels went into the Hearth roof. Its bed went into the hoarding around the Memorial ground. Its coulter sat on the Smithy floor for four days, because Ketil Brack could not think of one thing a coulter was worse at than what the Hold now wanted from it.

A coulter is the iron that runs ahead of the share and cuts the sod standing up. It is made to go down and to stay down. There is a curve in it for that.

He drew it out over the ninth day and the tenth. He did not take the curve all the way. It is still in the blade, near the spine, a hand above the tang, where the iron remembers what it was made to go into. Everybody who has since carried the Ashblade has put a thumb on that place. Nobody was ever taught to.

He worked alone and would not be watched. Bran Holt came twice to ask how long it would be and was sent off twice. On the evening of the tenth day Ketil carried it up to the Hearth in sacking, put it on the boards, said it was finished, and said it was not good.

Sixteen of power. Twenty-six of reach. An arc of a body and a half, which is to say that if two things stand where two things can work, one swing of it has them both.

He said those three before he said anything else about it, because they were the only parts of it he was sure of.

Somebody asked him whether it would hold an edge.

He said he would know in a fortnight and that he hoped to be wrong about it in either direction.

·  ·  ·

He wanted the count before he would let anyone hold it.

Sila Carver had a plumb line, a lead bob on waxed cord, which she used for setting the Memorial posts and for nothing else. Ketil hung it from the Smithy lintel at a hand and a half of cord and set it going. Out and back. Out and back. It does the same thing every time it does it, which is the whole of what a plumb line is for and the reason a Carver owns one.

He called one out and back a count. He cut the count into a hundred parts, because a hundred is a number you can cut a thing into and still say where in it you are. That is where the Vale's hundredths come from. There is no other reason for them, and there has never needed to be.

Then he put the blade in Rin Ashfall's hands and stood her in front of a fence stake and watched the bob and not her.

Ten hundredths to bring it up, he said. Ten in the meat. Twenty to get your hands back where they started.

Forty hundredths, he said, from deciding to being able to decide again.

He made her do it eleven times before he would say the number out loud to anybody else, and he counted the bob every time, and twice he made her stop because he had lost the count and would not guess at it.

Then he told her to swing hard.

Twenty-eight hundredths to bring it up. Fourteen in the meat. Thirty-eight to get your hands back. Eighty hundredths all told, and he said the eighty twice, and then he said the part that the Hold has been saying ever since.

Once it is coming up, it is coming. You have agreed to it. There is no halfway in it. You cannot put it down and choose again, and whatever walks into you while it is on its way walks into you, and it goes on walking into you for the thirty-eight after.

Rin Ashfall asked what she was to do about that.

Nothing, Ketil said. Agree to fewer of them.

She said she would rather agree to more of them and be quicker about it.

He said that was not a thing that could be arranged.

Then he said the other thing, and he said it badly, and he knew he was saying it badly.

He said that if two irons are coming at each other and you put yours into the path of the other one at exactly the moment it is spending itself, the spending comes back to you instead of going into the ground. He said he had felt it once at the fence stake on the eighth day and it had happened to him and he had not done it. He said he had no idea how a person would do it on purpose.

He said the window on it was twelve hundredths.

Rin asked him how he knew that if he had only had it once.

He said he did not know it. He said twelve hundredths was the narrowest thing he could measure with a bob on a string and that the window was narrower than the bob, and that until somebody made him a better string he was going to keep saying twelve and be honest about why.

Nobody in the Hold ever did it on purpose. It happened once more, eight days later, to somebody else, and she did not mean it either, and it decided the fight.

·  ·  ·

She was nineteen.

She swung first. Not fastest. First. In the whole of her life, in an argument, at a gate, over a spilled pot, the thing Rin Ashfall did was the thing she had done before she knew she had chosen it. Her mother had worked around it for nineteen years the way you work around a bad hinge, which is to say by always going through that door a little harder than the door needed.

And she came up off the ground cheap. She went down and came up and went down again and it took almost nothing out of her. Everybody in the Hold had seen her do it, in the hay and off the byre roof and out from under a slipped load of quarry stone on the fourth day, and nobody had thought about it, because there had been nothing yet in the Vale that made it worth thinking about.

For the other thing she carried there was no word on the tenth day. Sila Carver's hand puts it as *she has eight less of it than she ought and I do not know what it is.* She wrote that before anybody had a name for the thing she was eight short of. The name arrived the next afternoon and it arrived from her own pen, which she did not remark on and which is remarkable.

·  ·  ·

Three names were said at the Hearth on the evening of the tenth day and two of them were needed where they already stood.

Colm Grey had been a Warden since the third day and had asked for the blade outright at the start of the evening, which counted against him with four people and for him with three. Maren Verren had been made a Warden on the same third day and had spent eight of the nine days since in the lye vats and on the west furlong, because that was where the work was, and she was the only one who could do either without being told twice. Rin Ashfall had asked for the Warden's job on the third day and had been given the fields instead, by her own mother, in front of everybody.

That is the argument. It took most of a night.

Colm Grey's case was the one he had been making since the third day. He had walked out to the last fence twice on his own without telling anybody, which was the first thing anybody in this Chronicle ever did on their own without telling anybody, and he had opinions about the long grass, and he had said in front of the whole Hold that it did not need a second Warden and particularly not that one. He said now what he had said then, and then he said the new part, which was that the blade ought to go where the job already was.

Ketil Brack said the fence could not lose the only pair of eyes that had been past it and the fields could lose a body, and named which body, and it did not flatter anybody.

Maren Verren said nothing for the length of the argument. She was asked twice and she said she would do whatever the rota said, which is not a refusal and was not heard as one. Sila's hand records her sitting through the whole of it with a lye-burn on her wrist wrapped in sacking, not eating. Five days later she was standing in the same place saying the same sentence about the rota, and it was a different sentence by then, and everybody heard it differently.

Rin Ashfall's case, which she made once and did not make again, was that it had been her mother.

Bran Holt said that was not a reason and wrote it down anyway.

Bran Holt read the rota out. Nobody had asked him to and nobody stopped him. Fourteen of them, six jobs, the bread and the lye and the west furlong and the fence, and two of the three names standing under work the Hold could not put down, and the third standing under work that eleven other pairs of hands were already doing. He read it the way he read everything, from the top, including the parts nobody was arguing about, and it took him a long time, and by the end of it there was nothing left to say. So they said it for a while longer and then went to their beds.

That is a Succession. The Hold has held nine more of them since and not one of them has been about who could fight.

Sila's hand for that night is four lines long and three of them are about the wind.

·  ·  ·

On the morning of the eleventh day Ketil Brack set a post in the ground behind the Smithy and told her to work on it until he said stop.

She swung hard. She swung hard the first time, and she swung hard the second time, and she swung hard the third time, and the third one was slower.

He saw it in the bob before he saw it in her, and he said so, and she did not answer him because she was already bringing it up again.

The fourth swing was not there. Her arms went up with it and the iron did not arrive. It went into the post at the height of a man's knee, edge on, a hand span below where she had put the other three, and it stayed in the post, and she stayed holding it.

He told her to stop.

She did not stop, because she could not hear him. That is not a figure of speech and it should not be read as one. Her ears were working and his voice was going into them and it was arriving nowhere. She got the blade out of the post with two pulls and put her hands up for a fifth and stood there with her arms above her head and nothing in them at all.

Then she went down on one knee in the churned ground behind the Smithy and stayed there, and her right hand would not close, and she looked at it.

Ketil counted it out afterwards in front of the Hearth, on his fingers, to eleven people.

Four, he said. She has four of the hard ones. Eight of the light ones, or near enough to eight that I would not send anybody out on the ninth.

Somebody asked how long she had to stand still to get them back.

Not long, he said. But it does not come back all at once, and it does not come back at all while you are still swinging, and you do not get any of it back by wanting it back.

Then he said the thing he had been chewing since the fence stake on the eighth day.

He said a laid rope has the twist in it and the twist is not part of the weight. He said you can take the twist out of a rope and set it down in front of a man and the man will say it is the same rope, and it is the same rope, and it will not carry a bucket. He said that was what had gone out of her arms, and that it was not strength, because she was as strong at the fifth swing as she had been at the first, and it was not wind, because she was breathing. He said it was the twist and he had no word for the twist in a person.

Sila Carver had to write it in a column. A column wants one word.

She wrote *composure*, which is a word for a person keeping the arrangement they are in, and she wrote *poise* in the same line for the other thing, the packing a body wears and what it takes to open it, which Ketil had got at the fence stake three days before and had not named either.

So the Wardens of the Vale have been using a Carver's two words for the whole of their trade ever since, and not one of them has ever used either word to mean anything except what it meant that afternoon behind the Smithy. The Chronicle has them in the same line, in cramped letters, with the wax cut short at the end because she was running out.

·  ·  ·

Ketil moved the post three times that afternoon and would not say why until he had done it.

He set it behind the byre. She had four of the hard ones.

He set it inside the gate on the beaten ground. She had four.

He set it on the Memorial ground, at the foot of where Oda Ashfall was, because that was flat and he had run out of flat, and she had five and most of a sixth.

He made her do it again. Five and most of a sixth.

Bran Holt, who had been doing sums since the ninth day and had not stopped, said it was the same as the afternoon of the Burial, when the whole Hold had felt the work come easier at the same hour and nobody had been able to say why. Four people agreed with him. Two said the post was cheaper on the Memorial side because the ground there was turned and soft and she was not fighting her own feet in it, which is a good argument and nobody has ever disproved it. Both accounts are in the Chronicle. They do not agree and they were not made to.

Sila's hand: *The post is on the Memorial ground now. We have put the thing we hit on top of the thing we buried and I do not know what to write about that, so I am writing that it is cheaper there.*

The post is still there. It was still there on the two hundred and tenth day, with a hundred and ninety-nine days of iron in it and the bark long gone off the top third, standing at the foot of the oldest grave in the Vale, and by then nobody remembered it had ever stood anywhere else.

·  ·  ·

On the afternoon of the eleventh day Rin Ashfall took the blade.

There was no rite. Nobody had made one yet. There was a rite for this before the end of the month and it was made out of this afternoon, in the sense that people afterwards did what they remembered being done, which was not much.

Bran Holt said her name and said that the blade was hers, and did not say what her job was, because nobody had decided and nobody ever did.

That is worth being exact about, because the Hold got into the habit of it and never got out. Colm Grey was a Warden on the eleventh day and stayed one, and walked the fence for seventy-four days after that afternoon. Rin Ashfall was a field-hand on the rota, and on the twelfth day Bran Holt drew a line through her name in the field column and wrote nothing after it. There is no entry in the Chronicle giving her a job. There are two accounts of what she was called for the three days she had, and they do not agree, and the second of them was given to a Teller a very long time afterwards by somebody who had been a child on the eleventh day.

Ketil Brack put the blade in her hands and let go of it. Sila's hand says letting go took him a moment. Bran Holt's later copy does not mention it at all, and Bran Holt was standing closer.

Somebody said that she had taken the blade.

Sila's hand gives that to Bran Holt. Two people who were at the Hearth said afterwards that it was Ketil, and that he was talking about the iron and not about the job, and that he said it the way a man says a thing has been taken out of his shop. Both are in the Chronicle. It has not mattered which, because it is how the Vale has said it since. *Taking the blade.* *Taking the sword.* There is no other way of saying succession now in any valley a Wayfarer has walked into, and nobody has ever needed one, and it comes out of one sentence said at a fire by one of two men on the eleventh day.

She put it over her shoulder because she had nothing to hang it from. Ketil said he would see about that and he did see about that, two days later, and she wore it for two days on a hay rope.

·  ·  ·

The ledger board stood at the Hearth with one line on it. Sila cut the second that evening.

There were four columns of numbers on the board and none of the columns had a name. They had been there since the ninth day, when the Burial had come out at eight and four and five and nothing, and Bran Holt had worked out that six of something had been got for twenty of something spent, and had written both down, and had gone to bed unsatisfied and stayed that way.

Under the four columns there was one line. The line said *Drifting toward.* After it, in a cramped hand, it said *the quiet field.*

Nobody in the Hold knew who had put that line on the board. Sila Carver said she had not put it there. Bran Holt said he had not put it there. Ketil Brack said he could not write. It was there.

Rin Ashfall read it on her way past with the blade on the hay rope and asked which field.

Nobody answered her, because nobody knew, and because there were two fields and both of them were quiet.

·  ·  ·

The doorstone had been set on the fifth day and the dip in it was already there when they set it. It came off a byre threshold down the slope, worn out long and shallow in the middle by somebody else's feet over somebody else's years, and Oda Ashfall walked past the good squared flag Sila Carver had ready and went down the slope and came back up with two men and that stone instead, and set it dip side up, and stood on it, and said it would do.

She had one day of standing on it.

Her habit was the left foot. That was nineteen years old and it had belonged to doorways in a country nobody in the Hold has been back to, and it arrived at Ashfall Hold on the fifth day and was used twice.

From the eleventh day Rin Ashfall put two fingers on the doorstone on her way out. Not her foot. Her fingers, flat, into a dip worn by somebody the Hold had never met and would never have a name for, going down on one knee to reach it if she was in a hurry, and she was usually in a hurry.

Maren Verren never did it once. Not that day and not in the sixty-nine days she carried afterwards. That is worth setting down here and it will be worth something later.

She was the Hold's Warden from the afternoon of the eleventh day.

She had three days.
