# Chapter 4: What the Ash Does to the Dead

> *D7. Fair, wnd off mtn, no rain in it. She is coldr in ft & warmr in chst thn brd she lies on. Have cut this twice. Did nt blve my own hnd frst time.*
> Sila Carver, first hand, dated seventh day, cut on the ninth with the rest

**Hand:** Sila Carver
**Days:** 6 to 8
**Place:** Ashfall Hold, Wrenfield

On the sixth day they were driving the last fence at Wrenfield, where the near ground gives out and the long grass starts, and Oda Ashfall came down a slope of loose ash with a bundle of stakes on her shoulder.

The slope is rock under a hand's depth of grey. Everyone in the Hold knew it by then. Ketil Dunn had gone down there on the second day and left a thumbnail behind him. The ash takes a boot print and holds it like a seal, and then, where the rock runs close under it, the whole hand's depth goes out from beneath you at once, the way meal goes off a tilted board.

She went down on her left side with the stakes under her. She got up on her own. The first thing she said was about the stakes, which were good thorn, pointed the evening before at the fire, and she carried them the rest of the way to the line and set them in a stook, points up and butts out, the way you set stakes down so that they do not roll. Then she said that one of them had been in her.

It had gone in high inside the left thigh, a hand's breadth below the fork, and out again. There was not much on the outside of the cloth. Ketil Dunn said afterward that this was what fooled all of them, that a wound with nothing coming out of it looks like a wound you can walk on, and that she walked on it for four hundred paces and half of those uphill.

Wren Ashfall packed it. She was the Hold's Healer, which was the job nobody had wanted, and she was Oda Ashfall's younger sister, and she had a boiled cloth and lint out of the cart and a bowl of hearth-water. She got the leg up on a sack of the bad barley and she packed the hole and held it, and it filled. She took the packing out and packed it again with more, and it filled. The third time she put her whole hand into it to the wrist and held, and it went on filling around her hand. She said the words a Healer says, which are not many and are mostly about pressure.

They had carried Oda in over the doorstone.

The doorstone had been set on the fifth day, and it was the byre's threshold, worn in the middle in a long shallow dip by somebody else's feet over somebody else's years. Sila Carver had a good flag off the byre floor squared and ready and Oda Ashfall walked past it, went down the slope, and came back up with two men and the threshold instead. She set it dip facing up and stood on it and said it would do. Sila's entry for the fifth day is four words long and does not argue.

The blood ran into the dip and stood in it. It did not wash out. It has not washed out.

She was eleven hours dying and she was awake for most of them. She said nothing that anybody has since wanted repeated. She talked about work. She asked twice whether the stook of stakes had been set upright, because thorn butts left on the wet go soft. She said the lye vat wanted stirring and somebody ought to do it before the skin set on the top of it. She said that the west field wanted cutting before the wind turned.

An hour after that she stopped answering. A while after that she stopped.

There were fifteen people in Ashfall Hold that evening and then there were fourteen. The Hold was six days old. It had a Hearth with a fire in it and stone to hip height, a Smithy with a roof over half of it, a tarpaulin over the cart where the Carvers worked, a third of a palisade, a rope on hurdles where the rest of the palisade was going to be, a lye vat, a board on the Hearth wall with a rota on it in charcoal, and no rite.

That is not a failure of piety and should not be written as one. Of the fifteen, four could have said words over a body, and I have all four of those sets of words in three different hands, and no two of them agree on the order of anything. Two of the four came out of country where the dead go into fire. The third was Sable Kettle, whose people are under Kettlemere, where the dead go into water, and the water was down the slope and in sight of the door, and she said her people's words to herself at the cart that night and then said, out loud, that the person who had taught her those words was in the mere and could not be asked whether they were the right ones. The fourth said nothing at all and I have that fourth set of words from Bran Holt, who wrote them down eleven days later out of a conversation, and who does not say whose they were.

I record what was said. I do not settle it.

They laid her on the cart.

It was the flattest dry place in the Hold and the only one they could cover. The cart had come in with them, farm cart, cracked hub, and it stood on the north side of the Hearth out of the sun with a tarpaulin over a ridge pole above it, and that was where the Carvers worked and where Sila Carver's tool box lived. They took the box off and put it on the ground and they laid Oda Ashfall on the boards of the cart bed with sacking under her, and Sila brought the flap of the tarpaulin down over the tail of it and wedged it with a stake driven in at a slant.

Sable Kettle washed her. It took the clean water they had drawn that morning and two of the cloths, and she did it alone with the flap down, because she said it was not work to be watched. She was fourteen years old. Then she came out and went to the fire and banked it and sat by it until first light with her back against the stones, and Rin Ashfall sat on the other side of the fire, and neither of them, in any account I have, said one word to the other.

On the seventh day the body did not do what a body does.

Sila Carver's hand for that day is nine lines long and eight of them are weather. The ninth is the one I have set at the head of this chapter. Wren Ashfall came to the cart twice on the seventh day, in the morning and at dusk, which is the rota a Healer keeps for the living, and she kept it for her sister because nobody had told her to stop. What she did each time was this: she put the back of her right hand flat on the breastbone, held it there for a count, then put the same hand on the cart board, then on the Hearth's stone, then on her own breastbone. She did it in that order both times so that the order would mean something.

The board was cool. The stone was cool. Oda was warmer than either, and warmer at dusk than she had been in the morning.

There was no smell. There should have been the beginning of one by the second evening in that weather and there was none, and this is the thing Ketil Brack went back under the tarpaulin twice to satisfy himself about. The flies came. They came in the numbers flies come, and they stood along the edge of the tarpaulin and on the cart rail and on the spokes, thick enough that Asha Vane, who was fourteen and four days into being a Carver and had to get a graver out of the box on the ground, would not put her hand on the wheel. They did not go under the flap. Not one of them went under it. Asha Vane set that down in her own hand eighty-eight days afterward and it is the only thing she ever wrote about her own apprenticeship.

The rest of the seventh day the Hold worked, because there were fourteen of them and no slack anywhere. Bran Holt had begun, on the second day, to keep the work on sticks. He had six of them, one to a job, hazel, split flat on one face, and he cut a notch for a half-day given and a cross for a half-day lost, and he did it at dusk out of what people told him and not out of what he had seen, which he said was the only honest way to do it since he could not be in six places. On the seventh day he cut nine notches and eleven crosses. The crosses were the watch at the cart, which nobody had ordered and which four people kept anyway, in pairs, turn about, the way you sit up with a sick beast.

He kept those sticks for a hundred and eighty days and then he stopped, and the sticks went into the Hearth wall as chinking, and I have pulled two of them out, and the notches are still countable. I have counted them. They agree with his board to the half-day for every day I can check, which is most of them, and where they do not agree I have left both.

Also on the seventh day the cut on the heel of Bel Brack's hand, which he had got off the byre stone on the third day and which had done nothing whatever since, closed and began to scab. He showed it to two people. Neither of them said anything about it, and neither did he, and Sila Carver did not write it down.

She set, is the word Sila's hand uses. Not stiffened. A body stiffens and then lets go again and then goes soft, and every one of the fifteen had seen that happen, some of them to their own. Oda did not let go. Her hands had been open when Sable Kettle washed them, palms up, and by the afternoon of the seventh day they were not open. The fingers had come in to a curve, not a fist, the curve a hand makes around a haft it has known for thirty years, with a gap between the finger ends and the palm where the wood would be.

Ketil Brack straightened the left arm. He did it because he is a Smith and a Smith's way of knowing a thing is to put force into it and watch what the force does. He took the wrist and the elbow and laid the arm out flat along the cart board, and it went, without much argument, and he let go of it.

It came back. Not quickly. Over the space of a slow count of forty, with no sound to it, the elbow drew up and the forearm came across the body and the hand arrived where it had been, and the curve in the fingers was the same curve.

He did it again to be sure, and it came back again, and he went out and stood in the yard for a while and then went to the fire.

That evening two of them had to lift her off the cart bed and onto trestles, because the Carvers needed the cart. Ketil Brack took the shoulders and Ketil Dunn took the knees. They had carried her up the hill the day before between the three of them, her and Wren both, and they knew what she came to.

She was heavier.

Sila's hand says she was heavier. Bran Holt, copying that page eleven days afterward, wrote that she seemed heavier. I have kept Sila's word and set Bran's beside it here and I will not choose between them, though I will say that Bran Holt was not under the tarpaulin that evening and Sila Carver was standing at the flap.

There is no word in the first hand for what was on the trestles. The word the Hold uses now was not cut into this Chronicle until later and by another hand, and I have not gone back and put it into Sila's lines, though it would have made this chapter shorter to write and easier to read. Sila Carver did not have it. What she had was a woman's name, and she used it, and every person in that Hold used it, and they went on using it for three days.

On the morning of the eighth day the stake Sila had driven in at the flap was lying flat and the tarpaulin was thrown back over the ridge pole, folded, the way you fold it when you mean to come back to it.

Sable Kettle was up first, because she was always up first, and she was at the fire with the kettle in her hand when Oda Ashfall went past the Hearth door. Sable's account, which she gave me herself at that Hearth in the last season she had, is four sentences and I have not cut any of them.

She said: *She went past me on the outside of the door. She did not look in and she did not look out at the yard either. She walked like a woman who is late for something and has decided not to run. I said her name and she went on and I did not say it again.*

The rope on hurdles was the only thing between the Hold and Wrenfield at that hour, because two thirds of the palisade was stakes and the last third was rope. She went through the rope. She did not lift it and she did not go under it. She walked into it and it came off its peg and she walked out of it and it stayed on the ground behind her. That peg is still in that post. It is the only part of the first palisade still standing in its own hole.

She went down the headland to the west field.

The Hold had opened that field on the fourth day with hooks. A third of it was down in swathe and the rest was standing, and there was a strip of stubble about forty paces by eight where the cutting had stopped on the fifth evening.

She stood in the stubble and she began.

The motion is this. The left hand goes out and down and closes, gathering the standing grass in at about knee height, and draws it in toward the hip. The right hand comes across low, at shin height, in a flat arc from the right side of the body to the left, and the shoulders turn with it, and the weight goes from the right foot to the left. Then the left hand opens and lays what it is holding down to the left in a swathe with the heads all one way. Then the right foot comes up half a pace. Then it begins again.

She had nothing in either hand.

The left hand closed on nothing and drew nothing in. The right hand came across at the height a hook comes across and there was no hook in it. Nothing was cut. Nothing was laid down. The grass she reached into was the grass she had already reached into, because she did not go up the field. She worked the same three paces of stubble, forward half a pace and back, forward and back, and the stubble inside those three paces went down flat and bent inward.

At the end of every fourth pass she straightened, and put her right hand out to the side at the height of her hip, and drew the edge of what was not in her hand along a cloth that was not at her belt, twice, once each way. Then she stooped again.

She had done that with a hook in her hand every fourth pass for as long as anybody in the Hold had known her. Ketil Dunn had worked a field beside her for two summers in another valley and said he could have given you the count without watching her, and did, and was right.

Fourteen people came out onto the headland and stood on it. Nobody gave an order. Wren Ashfall came last, because she had gone to the trestles first to see what was not on them.

Rin Ashfall went out.

She was nineteen and she went before anybody had decided anything, which is what she did, and Maren Verren went after her and stopped three paces behind her and did not touch her. Rin walked down into the stubble and stood at about two paces off, on the side the right hand swings away from, which is the side you stand on when you want to talk to somebody who is cutting.

She said, Mother.

The motion did not change. Not the count, not the height of the arc, not the half step.

She said it twice more and then she said the whole name, which is what people do when a name on its own has not worked, and the fourth pass came round and the right hand went out to the side at hip height and drew along the cloth that was not there, twice, once each way, and Rin Ashfall was standing where the cloth would have been.

The back of the hand came across her forearm. It was not a blow and nobody has ever claimed it was. It was a hand going to where a cloth hangs, and her arm was in the way of it. Rin went back two steps with her forearm across her chest and her mouth open, and Maren Verren had her by the shoulders from behind and walked her backward up the headland the whole way without either of them turning round.

The mark was on her arm for six days. It was the shape of two knuckles. She had it when she took the blade and she had it when she died, and Sila's hand mentions it both times.

Ketil Brack did not go down into the field and did not send anybody into it. He walked the headland from one end of the west field to the other, about two hundred paces, looking at her the way he looks at a weld, from one side and then from the other, and came back and said to Bran Holt that she was carrying it in the hips and the shoulders and that whatever was in her was in her the way lead is in a stock, all through and all the way to the middle. Bran Holt asked him what he meant by that. He said he would know better once he had gone at her. Bran Holt said nobody was going at her. Ketil Brack did not answer him, and Bran Holt wrote that down.

That is the eighth day's argument in full. There was more of it and it went on past dark, and I have three accounts of the words and not one of them survives the others intact, and all three end with the same two men not answering each other.

They stood on the headland until the middle of the day and then, in ones and twos, they went back to work, because the lye vat had a skin on it and the fence had eleven stakes left to drive and there were fourteen people to feed. Colm Grey stayed. He was one of the two Wardens and he had asked for the job and got it on the third day, and what he did with the eighth was stand at the top of the slope with his arms folded and watch a woman cut nothing for eight hours. He did not go down and he did not sit. When he came in he said one thing and then ate, and the thing he said was that she was not tired.

She was not. That is the fact of the eighth day and everything the Vale has done since comes out of it. She did not slow. She did not straighten and put her hands into the small of her back. She did not stop at the end of a swathe and look at what she had laid down. She went on at the pace of a woman with a field to finish, in the dark, through the night. They could hear the half step from inside the Hold. Ash under a boot has a sound to it, and the sound came at the same distance apart all night, and Bran Holt lay on his back in the dark and counted them and stopped at nine hundred because he had lost his place and did not want to start again.

Sila Carver wrote nine words at the foot of the eighth day and then began a tenth line four times and left it.

*It is not her. It is her hold, and I do not know which is worse to write.*

Bran Holt's copy has that line. Asha Vane's copy has it. The Wayfarer copy that came back to us out of Holt Vale has it, in a hand not ours, with the last five words dropped. Somebody two ridges east decided the line was better short and read it out that way, and I have heard it said that way by people who have never stood in this valley.

I have put it back the way she wrote it. I record what was said. I do not settle it.

## Ledger

None. The board had been on the Hearth wall since the fourth day with nothing on it but a rota in charcoal, and no line was cut into it for the sixth, seventh or eighth day, because there was no verb for any of them. There are eighteen verbs and there have only ever been eighteen. The first line was cut on the ninth day, and the four numbers were put to it later and by another hand, back-counted by a man who never said by what rule.
