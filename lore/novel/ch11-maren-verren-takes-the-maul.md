# Chapter 11: Maren Verren Takes the Maul

> *Three names were called. Two of them were needed.*
> Recorded: the board, day fifteen, in Sila Carver's hand

**Hand:** Sila Carver, first hand
**Days:** 15
**Place:** the Hearth, the Smithy

The Hold held its first Succession on the fifteenth day, and it was not a rite then and
nobody called it one. It was a meeting about who was going to do which work, held in a
room with a cup on the table that nobody would move.

Everything that a Succession is now was in that meeting already, which is the thing I find
hardest to write about it. The three names. The arithmetic said out loud in front of the
body. The bond counted as a quantity. All of it was there on the fifteenth, invented by
twenty-two frightened people in one afternoon, and none of it has been improved since,
only repeated.

·  ·  ·

Three names were called, and Sila Carver wrote all three, which is the only reason we know
two of them.

**Bran Holt.** Fifty-one. Warden by the rota and tally-keeper by temperament, with a hip
that had been wrong since before the Vale and a hand that would become the second hand in
five days. He could read ground better than anyone in the Hold. He could not run.

**Ketil Brack.** Thirty-eight. Smith. He had made the Ashblade and he had made the hinges
and he had made the graver Sila cut the panes with, and he was the only person in the Vale
who could do any of those things. Taking him out of the Smithy would have cost the Hold
its iron, its glass and its tools in one stroke, and every person in that room could do
that sum.

**Maren Verren.** Twenty-six. Field-hand. Married to the dead woman since the spring
before the Vale. Hands that did not shake, which was a fact about her that people
mentioned before they mentioned anything else, and a flat scar on the forearm from
something that had happened before she came to the Hold and that she never explained. She
was needed in the fields the way everybody was needed in the fields, which is to say she
was needed and it was survivable.

That was the whole of the choosing. There is no scene of deliberation to record because
there was no deliberation. Bran Holt said the two sums, the iron sum and the running sum,
and then there was one name left, and everyone in the room had arrived at it before he
finished and had waited for him to finish out of politeness.

Sila's entry for the choosing is four words: *the fields can bear it.*

·  ·  ·

What nobody in the room said, and what everybody in the room understood, is that Maren
Verren was the worst possible choice and would therefore be the best one.

The Hold had a fortnight of evidence by then and it had all come from one field. Oda
Ashfall had died and something had gone out of the doorstone and into the ground under
her, and when the Hold buried her whole, with her knife, the ground gave it back and
everyone in the Hold felt the giving as a lightness in the arms that lasted the rest of the
afternoon. They had no theory. They had a great many observations and a rule of thumb,
and the rule of thumb was this: a person's grip is somewhere, and if it is not in the
ground and not shared out then it is out in a field holding a corpse up.

Rin Ashfall's grip was in the long grass past the last fence. So, in a way the Hold had no
language for and I still do not, was a good deal of Maren Verren's.

She had less to spend than she should have had. Anyone who watched her on the sixteenth
could see it and nobody could explain it, and the explanation the Wardens settled on years
later is the only one that has ever fitted: what she had was not all with her. Part of it
was lying out there in the bent grass, closer to the thing in the grass than to her, and
the thing in the grass was holding it, and she was not going to get it back by wanting to.

And against that, the other half of the same fact. She could not be moved. Not out of a
line, not off a decision, not off the swing she had committed to even when committing was
plainly going to cost her the ribs. The Hold noticed within the week that she would take a
blow in order to finish a stroke, and would do it again the next day, and did not appear
to be doing it out of grief or spite but out of a kind of arithmetic she had done once and
was not going to revisit.

Less in the purse. Nothing that could be taken out of her. Both of those are one
sentence, and the sentence is that most of what she was, was already out in the field, and
what was left was the part that had decided things.

The Hold read this in her within four days. It has read it in every spouse since. The
words said at a Succession now, when a candidate has shared a roof with the dead, are
*they shared a roof with the dead*, and they are said flatly, as a fact about the
candidate's capacity, the way a rota-keeper says a man has a bad hip.

I have heard them said at eleven Successions. They are never said unkindly and they are
always said in front of the family.

·  ·  ·

The clock ran while they talked.

This is the part of the fifteenth day that I have gone back to more than any other, and it
is the part Sila Carver did not think worth a line, and I do not blame her, because on the
fifteenth day it was not yet a horror. It was a piece of arithmetic they had not finished
doing.

They had worked out on the eleventh that nothing in the Hold advanced while everyone was
inside it. Bread that had not risen in the morning had not risen in the evening. A pot
left off the fire was the same temperature at dusk. It was convenient, on the whole. Bran
Holt had already started using it: he held his tally sessions in the Hold and went out to
count.

What they had not yet worked out on the fifteenth is that the clock does not belong to the
Hold. It belongs to whoever is outside.

Two Foragers were at the Stonewake kilns that day, burning shell for lime. They had gone
out on the thirteenth and they came back on the sixteenth and they had not heard about any
of it. Three days of their walking, which is three days of the world.

So while twenty-two people sat in a room and moved no bread and did not touch a cup, the
world outside the palisade went forward, because two people who did not know anything were
out in it turning shell in a kiln.

Rin Ashfall's Echo grew a growth on the sixteenth and another on the eighteenth, and the
Hold has always dated those growths from the deaths and not from the burning of shell,
and it has always been wrong to do so, and I have not corrected the older entries because
correcting them would be a lie of a different kind.

Sila wrote nothing about this. Bran Holt worked it out on the twentieth, in his first week
with the hand, and wrote four lines about it, and the four lines are the reason the Hold
has ever since counted the days a body has been lying out rather than the days since it
fell. Those are usually the same number. On the fifteenth day of the Hold they were not,
and the difference was a day, and the difference cost Maren Verren more than anything else
that happened that week.

·  ·  ·

She would not have the Ashblade, and it was not a decision, because the Ashblade was in
the grass in her wife's hands.

Ketil Brack, who had spent the previous evening offering to forge another and being told
no, tried once more on the morning of the fifteenth and was told no in the same word, and
then he asked her, reasonably, what she proposed to carry.

She took him out to the Smithy yard and showed him the quarry sledge.

It was not a quarry sledge yet. The Hold had no quarry on the fifteenth day; it had
Stonewake and a notion. What it had was a sledge Ketil had made in the first week for
driving palisade posts, a squared head on a long ash haft, and the head was iron and it
was heavy and it was the heaviest thing in the Vale that a person could pick up and swing
once.

Ketil said it was a post-driver.

She said she knew what it was.

He said a post-driver cannot be recovered from, and he said it as a smith, not as a
Warden, because the Wardens did not exist yet as a body of opinion. What he meant, and he
was exactly right, is that a thing that heavy takes a long time to arrive and an even
longer time to be finished arriving, and that in the gap between those two, a person
holding it is furniture.

The number the Wardens use now is forty-six hundredths of a second from the decision to
the landing, going heavy, and half a second again afterwards in which the world may do as
it likes with you. I did not know that number when I was cutting panes and I know it now
because I have held the thing, and I will say that the number does not convey it. What
conveys it is that you commit to a swing of the Maul while looking at something that is
not yet where it will be when the head arrives, and you are not permitted to change your
mind, and nothing in the Vale has ever taught a person more about their own character.

Against that: twenty-four. Against that, an arc that takes two bodies if they are standing
badly. Against that, a head that does not care what it hits first.

Ketil reforged it in a day and a half. He drew the head down, dished the striking face,
and set it back on the haft with a wedge instead of a pin so that it could be reset in the
field, which was the first piece of design in the Vale's history that was made because a
person was expected to be out for days.

He also, without asking, ground a short edge into the trailing side. He never explained
it. She used it twice in sixty-nine days and both times it saved her, and neither time did
she mention it to him, and he never asked.

·  ·  ·

The naming happened on the sixteenth in the yard, in front of nine or ten people, and it
happened in about four seconds, and it cannot be taken back.

Bel Brack, eleven years old and up on something he should not have been up on, which was
where he spent that whole month, said: *that is a widow's maul.*

There is no record of anybody laughing. There is a record, from two people, that Ketil
Brack put his hand on the back of the boy's neck, which in Ketil's vocabulary meant be
quiet immediately.

Maren Verren looked at the boy and then at the weapon and said nothing at all, and picked
it up, and went out.

That is the naming. The Hold had it by the evening and the Chronicle had it by the
twentieth in Bran Holt's hand as *Widow's Maul* with no note of where the name came from,
and Bran knew perfectly well where it came from, and left it out, and I have put it back.

She could have stopped it. She had the standing to stop it and everyone would have
obeyed. She let it stand. Two people have told me she let it stand because she thought the
boy had said something true and there was no sense in a Hold of twenty-two people
pretending otherwise. One person has told me she let it stand because she wanted the thing
to be called what it was every time anybody picked it up after her, including everybody
who would pick it up after her. I record what was said. I do not settle it.

The Maul has been carried by two wielders since. Tarn Stonewake took it off the wall on the
hundred and twenty-second day, which the Hold read as arrogance and which was the opposite
of arrogance. Nobody has carried it since the hundred and fortieth. It is on the wall
above the Hearth as I write this and the wedge in the haft is the one Ketil cut, and it
has been reset nine times in the field, and you can count the seatings.

·  ·  ·

She did not touch the doorstone.

I have thought about whether to put this in, because it is the kind of detail a Teller can
lean on until it breaks, and because I am the fourth hand and everything I have of the
fifteenth day is second hand and forty years cold. But three people who were in the yard
that morning told me the same thing without being asked, and the third of them was Sila Grey,
who was a girl in that yard and is sixty-one now and has spent the whole of the years
between arguing that a Hold should stop saying its dead out loud, and Sila Grey told me it
twice, twenty years apart, in the same words.

Rin Ashfall touched the doorstone every time she went out. Two fingers flat on the worn
dip, without slowing.

Maren Verren went past it.

She went past it on the sixteenth and on the seventeenth and on every one of the sixty-nine
days she held the Maul, out and back, across most of a working life, and Sila Grey watched
her do it for the first several summers the way a child watches a thing she has noticed and
does not understand, and then stopped watching because it had become ordinary, which is
what happens.

I am not going to say what it meant. Sila Carver did not say. Bran Holt did not record it
at all. What I will say is that the dip in that stone is warmer than the stone around it,
and that it has been warmer since the sixth day of the Hold, and that it is warm now, and
that every Teller has checked and every Teller has written down that they checked and none
of us has ever written down what we think.

·  ·  ·

Sila cut the entry that evening.

Nobody had a verb for it yet. She wrote what had happened in the plainest words she had,
and the plainest words she had were that Maren Verren had taken up the blade, which was
not even accurate, because the blade was in the grass and what Maren had taken up was a
post-driver.

The phrase stuck anyway. *Took up the blade.* It is the verb on the board to this day, in
all four hands, for all ten of them, including the two who never carried a sword, and it
has been said over the bodies of nine people, and the tenth is me.

Four numbers moved. Two of them by nothing at all.

Sila did not understand the shape of what she was writing. None of the first hand does.
She wrote the day, the thing done, and four figures, in a cramped hand with the letters cut
short to save wax, and then she put the graver down and went to sit with Maren Verren, who
was at the table, with the cup.
