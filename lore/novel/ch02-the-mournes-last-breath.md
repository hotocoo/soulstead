# Chapter 2: The Mourne's Last Breath

> *Fall-ash drinks. Drawn-ash will not drink. Learn it with your thumb, because out here your eye is no use to you at all.*
>
> the Forager's test, as it is said at Ashfall Hold, and as Sable Kettle said it to me twice

**Hand:** Grieve Mourne, compiling, out of Sable Kettle's telling, Bran Holt's second hand, and four Wayfarer copies · **Days:** before the first, and the fifth season of anywhere · **Place:** the Mourne, Wrenfield, Stonewake Scarp

The Mourne stands at the north head of the valley and does nothing at all.

It is a fire-mountain and it is dead. The north shoulder has fallen away, so the notch in it faces down the valley and you can see into the throat from the fields, which is a grey hollow with a floor of loose stuff and no water in it. There are vents on the east flank, and if you put your hand over one on a cold morning there is a breath of warm air on your palm that smells the way two flints smell when you strike them. That is all it does. It has done that every morning of my life. Snow will not lie on the east flank and lies on the west, and the line between the two runs straight down the mountain like a seam in cloth, and in February you can see it from the Hearth step.

Before that it breathed once, properly, and laid a hand's depth of grey ash over everything in this valley.

Nobody wrote it down. The people who could write were under it. What is left is what the ash itself says, and the ash says a great deal if you dig, which the Carvers do when they want clean sand. Four fingers down through the grass mat and you are into it, and it is the same everywhere: one layer, even, graded fine at the top and coarse at the bottom, so it fell in one go and not in twenty. It went over the roofs, because there are roof slates in the middle of that layer lying flat where the timbers gave out under the weight. It went into the byres, because there are the bones of penned animals in it, standing, all facing one way. It went onto the lake, and Sable Kettle says her people had it out of their grandmothers that the mere took a week to come clear and that everything in it came up dead and floated and then sank. And it filled the worn dip in every doorstone in this valley, level, so that the first thing anybody did anywhere, afterwards, was sweep a doorstep.

How long ago is not settled and I cannot settle it. Of the four Wayfarer copies I have, one says nine years and gives a season. One says ninety and gives nothing. One does not mention the Mourne at all and starts with the ash already on the ground as though it had always been there, which is the honest position and I respect it. The fourth says it fell twice and that the second fall was thin, and there is no second layer anywhere I have dug, and that copy is in a good hand and has been carried a long way and I have no reason to call a stranger a liar. I record what was said.

What is settled is that this valley has been come into more than once. Low Kettle is under the water and Low Kettle was a village with a mill. Vane Hold is behind its own wall at the south end and Vane Hold had kilns going all summer. We are the third that I can prove and I would not be surprised by a fourth. Nobody came here because they were driven. They came here because the ground is the best ground in the world, and that is the thing about this valley that I want written down plainly in one place, and this is the place.

· · ·

Ash ground tills like flour.

I have put a spade into ordinary ground and into ash ground in the same week and the difference is not small, it is comic. Ordinary ground you lean on. Ash ground takes the spade to the shoulder on the weight of your arm, and comes up in a heap that will not hold a clod, and runs off the blade. You do not break ash ground, you move it. A woman can turn a furlong in a day and stand up straight at the end of it. Two children with a hoe can make a seed bed that would take four adults anywhere else.

It is warm. Not hot, and not from the mountain, whatever the burners say: it is warm the way a haystack is warm, from underneath, and the crust of it thaws two weeks before the clay at the wood edge does. So you sow early. You sow while other valleys are waiting, and you are up and green before the birds have worked out where to be.

And it holds water. This is the part that makes people sell their roofs and walk. Ash ground takes a downpour and keeps it, all of it, and gives it back slowly for six weeks, so that a dry August that would empty a barn anywhere else does nothing to you at all. Sable Kettle's word for it was that ash ground is a sponge with a grudge. You cannot drown it and you cannot dry it.

The first crop out of it is not believable. I have seen the first crop off new ground twice, once on the strip the Carvers opened east of the workshop and once at Brackwood where the burners had a garden. The barley comes up so thick you cannot see soil between the rows by the third week. The straw is soft and pale and too long, so it goes over in wind, which is the only complaint anybody makes and they make it happily. The ears are heavy enough that a full sheaf has to be carried on the hip, not under the arm. Ketil Dunn, who was Orrin Dunn's father, and who had the eastern furlong before he gave it to his son, stood in the middle of the first Wrenfield harvest with his hands on his knees and said that he had been farming for thirty years and had been doing it wrong.

The second crop is as good. Sometimes it is better, because by then you know where the wet ends of the field are.

The third is a little less, and nobody says anything, because the third is still better than anywhere any of you have ever lived.

The fourth is less again. That is the year people start to explain it. They explain it with the weather, and with the seed, and with the man who has the next furlong along and does not keep his ditch clear.

· · ·

The fifth year the fields are hungry again.

The words are exactly those and they are not anybody's invention, they are what is said, and I have heard them said at the Hearth about a single furlong in a single week and watched the room go quiet at them the way a room goes quiet at a name.

Here is what it looks like, because the looking is the whole horror of it. The soil is the same soil. It is the same colour, dark grey with a brown cast when it is wet. It smells the same, which is faintly of a cold hearth. It tills the same, like flour, off the blade. Dig it to the shoulder and there is nothing wrong with it: no sour water, no pan, no rot, worms where worms should be. Take a handful and you cannot tell it from a handful off the strip that yielded to the hip four seasons ago, and neither can anybody else, and Foragers have tried.

The barley comes up. That is the cruelty of the fifth year: it comes up. It comes up pale at the base and stays short, and the leaf is the wrong green, a yellow green from the tip back, and it heads early because a plant that is failing will always try to seed before it dies. Crush an ear in your fist and you get chaff and dust and two grains the size of sand. The field stands there green to the eye from the fence and it is empty, and a man can walk out to it every morning for three weeks telling himself it will fill.

The word is locked ground.

That is Sable Kettle's word and she had it off her mother, who had it out of Low Kettle before the water, and what her mother said, and what Sable said to me in the Hearth with a cup in her hand, was this: it is all still in there. The goodness is in there. It is in there and the roots cannot get hold of it. Something in the ash has closed its hand around it and will not open.

I have no better account than that and I will not go past it. I have heard Healers offer three explanations and I have heard a Wayfarer offer a fourth out of a valley where they say the ash is alive, and that one I did not write down in the Chronicle because it is not a thing anybody saw. Locked ground is a thing anybody can see. You can see it from the fence.

· · ·

The answer is bone, and it was learned hard, by people who are not here to be thanked for it.

Ground bone, first. Any bone: the beasts you kill, the beasts that die, the boiled-out ends of anything that went into a pot. It is broken on the anvil at the cold end of the Smithy and then ground between two stones, which is long, stupid, hand-numbing work that the Hold gives to whoever has offended it. Second, burnt shell out of Kettlemere. The mere is thick with a small ridged mussel and in winter when the water is down you can rake a barrow of them off the shallows in an afternoon. Third, lye. The lye is drawn off the ash of our own pyres, steeped in water in the vats behind the Hearth and taken off clear, and what is left in the bottom of the vat after the lye has been drawn goes in as well.

All of it goes back into the furrows. A handful to a yard, broadcast by hand, and turned in before the first frost so the winter can work at it. It does not fix a locked field in one season. It fixes it in two, and it keeps a field that is not yet locked from locking, which is why it is done every autumn on every strip whether the strip needs it or not, and why the argument about who has to do the grinding is the longest-running argument in this Hold and will outlive me.

The shell and the bone are burnt at Stonewake before they are ground, in the lime-kilns under the quarry.

There are four kilns and three of them are drawn at any time. They are stone bottles dug into the bank, a fire door at the foot and a mouth at the top, and you charge them in layers, a foot of broken limestone, a foot of wood, a foot of stone, and you light them and you do not let them go out for nine days. What comes out of the bottom is quicklime, which will take the skin off your wrist if you are careless with it and the water off your eye if you are careless twice. Everything within a hundred feet of the kilns is white. The grass is white, the men are white, the dog at the top kiln is white and has been white for as long as I have been alive, and they all work with a rag tied over the mouth and nose and they talk in the short way people talk through cloth.

Tarn Stonewake drew the top kiln. He was not of the Hold and he did not come down to it, not once, for a hundred and twenty-one days. On the hundred and twenty-second he came down and took up the Maul, and what that cost is in its own chapter, and the Hold never did learn his mother's name.

I will say the plain form of this once and then leave it where it is. A Hold feeds its fields its dead. The bone in the furrow is the bone of the beasts and the lye in the furrow is the ash of the people, and the barley that comes up out of it is what the Hold eats in the winter. This is not a figure of speech. It is a thing you do in the third week of October with a shovel and a sack, and then you go in and have your supper.

· · ·

There are two ashes and they are the same colour.

Fall-ash is what the Mourne laid down. Fall-ash grows barley. Drawn-ash is what the ground becomes in the places where something was taken out of it and not put back, and drawn-ash grows nothing and never will. That last is not a Forager saying it hard for effect. It is measured. There is a strip at the west end of Wrenfield that has been sown four times by four different people who each thought the others had been careless, and nothing has come up out of it, not barley, not weed, not thistle, and thistle will grow on a roof.

You cannot tell them apart by looking. I want to be exact about that, because people who have not been out there assume there is some obvious difference and that Foragers are making a craft out of nothing. There is no difference to the eye. Same grey, same fineness at a glance, same way of banking against a fence.

Here is the test, as Sable Kettle gave it to me, in her order, which she said was the order that matters.

First, take your handful from four fingers down and not off the top, because the top of anything is only weather.

Second, spit in it. Fall-ash drinks. It darkens in a moment and the mark spreads. Drawn-ash will not drink. It beads, and the bead sits there on the surface looking at you, and you can tip the handful and the bead will roll off it and the ash under it will be dry.

Third, press it with your thumb. Fall-ash has grit in it and little grains of light stuff you can feel between the pads of your fingers, and it gives and springs back. Drawn-ash is finer than pot-ash. It takes the print of your thumb whole, every line of it, and it keeps the print, and if you come back at evening the print is still there.

Fourth, stop and listen, and count to twenty while you do it. In fall-ash there are insects. In drawn-ash there is nothing, and what you will notice is not the silence, it is that you had already noticed the silence a hundred paces back and had not told yourself.

Fifth, look up. In drawn-ash the trees stand dead and upright and do not come down, because nothing arrives to bring them down. Standing deadwood with the bark still on is worth more to a Forager than any handful.

And last, and slowest, and the only one that is certain: see what grows. Fall-ash grows barley. Drawn-ash grows nothing. If you can wait a season you do not need the other five.

The Foragers had the five words for how far a place has gone before the board ever had them, and the board took them off the Foragers and not the other way about. Quick, for ground that is green and wet and loud with insects. Turning, when the grass goes pale at the root and there are fewer birds. Greying, when the ash is in the seams of everything and the colour is going out of the whole place. Ashen, when the drawn-ash lies over the soil and the trees are standing dead upright. And Gone, which is no sound, no growth, ash to the ankle, and the route closed.

Sable Kettle would not run the test in the Grey Ash. She said so out loud at the Hearth, more than once, in front of people who wanted her to say something else: that under the Mourne the two ashes are mixed past telling apart, that the thumb print test fails there because everything there takes a print, and that any Forager who tells you they can read the Grey Ash is a Forager who has not been far enough into it. The Cull have been repeating that sentence ever since, in a different tone than she used, and they are not misquoting her.

· · ·

The mountain is not answerable to anybody.

It killed a valley in an afternoon, and then gave the valley back as the best ground that anyone in it had ever put a spade into, and then took it back again by arithmetic over five quiet seasons in which nothing whatever happened. There is no argument to have with it. It has a warm breath on the east flank and a seam of snow down the west and it will still be doing that when this book is shelved, and the four numbers on the board will not move it a hair either way.

What can be argued with is us. So I have put the ash at the front of this, before the founding and before the first name, because every single thing that follows is a consequence of fifteen people looking at a hand's depth of grey ash and being right about it.
