# Chapter 10: The Day the Hillside Was Beautiful

> *Rin Ashfall. Nineteen. Four days with the blade. Twenty-five.*
> Recorded: the second pane, west range, cut by Sila Carver on day fifteen and never recut

**Hand:** Sila Carver, first hand, with two villager testimonies I have kept both of
**Days:** 14
**Place:** Wrenfield, past the last fence

Rin Ashfall had the blade for four days. She went out on the eleventh, the twelfth and
the thirteenth, and on the fourteenth she went out again and did not come back, and that
is the whole of her holding of it, and everything else in this chapter is furniture.

I put it plainly here because Sila put it plainly there. Her entry for the fourteenth is
six words and a number. I have read hands since that would have made a page of it, mine
among them, and none of them improved on the six words.

·  ·  ·

The morning was cold and clear and the ash had a crust on it from the night, which the
Foragers liked, because a crust means the ground has been still.

She ate standing. That was a habit her mother had and she had it worse. Maren Verren sat
at the table with her hands round a cup and said nothing worth writing down, and Bran Holt
came in with the tally board and said that the long grass past the last fence had put on
a foot since the turn of the season and that things were coming out of it into the near
rows at night and taking the tops off the barley.

Rin asked what he wanted her to do about grass.

Bran said he wanted the grass cut. He said it in the voice a man uses when he is asking
for the thing he actually wants and pretending it is the small thing next to it.

Ketil Brack was at the fire by then, and he put in that the Ashblade was not a scythe and
would not thank anybody for being used as one. He had made it out of a plough coulter and
a cart's iron nine days before and he was still not proud of it. He said the edge was
holding better than he had any right to expect, which was his way of saying that he had
been frightened of it failing.

Nobody in that room said do not go. I have asked four people who were in it. Nobody said
it. Two of them told me they had thought it, which is not the same and does not go on the
board.

She touched the doorstone on the way out. She did that every time, the flat of two
fingers on the worn dip where her mother used to stand to watch the weather, and she did
it without slowing down or looking at it, the way you touch the lintel of a door you have
gone through ten thousand times. Maren Verren watched her do it that morning and thought
nothing of it. I have that from Maren directly, years later, and she was quite firm about
the nothing.

·  ·  ·

Wrenfield in the fourteenth day of the Hold was mostly green. This is worth saying because
nobody now remembers it green.

The near rows were barley on ash ground in its second season, which is to say the best
barley anyone in that company had ever grown, thick and heavy-headed and standing up
straight. Past the near rows there was the last fence, four rails of Brackwood ash and
rope. Past the fence the grass went on for what a body could walk in half a morning, and
in the fourteenth day it was up past the waist and in places up past the chest, and the
wind moved through it in long slow shapes like something under a blanket turning over.

The slope on the west side ran up to a cut bank where the Kettle had been before it
changed its mind, and the bank was the good ground, and the good ground was why there was
loose ash under the grass at the foot of it, brought down by the winter and hidden all
spring. A Forager would have told her. The Forager who would have told her was at the
kilns that morning, burning shell.

She went past the fence at the middle of the morning. Sila's entry says the middle of the
morning. Bran's later copy says early. I have both and I am not going to choose between a
man with a tally board and a woman with a graver, both of whom were looking at other
things.

The clock starts at the fence. Everyone in the Vale knows this now, and on the fourteenth
day of the Hold they knew it too, because they had worked it out on the eleventh and
argued about it on the twelfth and by the thirteenth they were using it: a minute out
there is an hour of the world, and while she was walking in the grass the Hold behind her
was not moving at all. Twenty-two people at their work, and none of the work advancing,
and the bread not rising, and the fire not going down.

She cut grass for a while. There is a testimony from Bel Brack, who was a boy then and up
the ladder on the palisade frame where he was not supposed to be, that she cut for a long
time and cut badly, in short savage strokes, standing still and turning at the waist, and
that at one point she stopped and put her hands on her knees.

A sword is not a scythe. Ketil had told her that inside the hour.

·  ·  ·

The first one came out of the grass at her left side and she killed it with one swing.

I want to be exact about this because the Hold got a great deal of use out of being exact
about it afterwards. A Cinder Stalker gives you a quarter of a second between deciding and
arriving. That is the whole of the warning, and it is not really a warning, it is more
like the noise a thing makes on its way. What you can do in a quarter of a second is
finish something you have already started.

She had already started. She was mid-stroke on the grass and the stroke went through the
grass and through the thing behind it and the thing came apart at the shoulder and was
dead before it had properly decided to be alive. Bel Brack says it made no sound. Ketil
Brack, who was not there, says they always make a sound and the boy was too far away to
hear it. I record what was said. I do not settle it.

The second came from the right and she was in recovery.

That is the word the Wardens use now and they got it from this afternoon. A swing is a
thing you agree to before it happens, and when it is done there is a piece of time
afterwards in which you are not able to do anything at all, and with the Ashblade going
light that piece is a fifth of a second, and a fifth of a second is nothing until the
moment when it is the only thing there is.

She took it across the ribs. Twelve, they would say later, when they had worked out how to
say such things. Twelve of what a body has.

She killed the second one too. It took her three strokes because she was moving badly on
that side, and by the third stroke she was going slower, and she felt herself going
slower, which is a thing everyone who has held the blade since has described in almost the
same words and which nobody has ever described well.

The Wardens say Composure. They say it flatly, as a tradesman says plumb. What is meant is
that a swing is paid for out of a purse you carry, and the purse does not fill while you
are spending from it, and it does not begin to fill the moment you stop, either. There is
a wait. On the fourteenth day of the Hold nobody had a word for it and Rin Ashfall was
standing in chest-high grass on loose ash finding it out in person.

·  ·  ·

The third and the fourth came together.

Ketil Brack's account, which he gave many times and which hardened as he gave it, is that
there were four. Bran Holt's copy says three, and Bran Holt was writing from what he was
told and had no reason to shave one off, and Bel Brack from the palisade frame said four
and then said he had only clearly seen three and had assumed the fourth from the way she
turned. Sila's own entry gives no number.

Four is what the Hold settled on. I am not the Hold and I am not settling it. What I can
say is that there were more than two and that they came from the downhill side, which is
the side where the loose ash was, and that when she stepped back off the strike she
stepped onto ground that was not ground.

She went down to one knee. Not from a blow. From the ash.

Here is the part that the Vale has kept, and it has kept it because it is the only part
that is any use. She had nothing left in the purse. A body with nothing left can still
move; it simply moves like a body. The roll that would have carried her out from under
them, that thing every wielder after her has learned as a quarter of a second in which
the world cannot touch you inside half a second in which you can do nothing else, was not
available. What was available was a slow sidestep, and a slow sidestep from one knee, on
ash, with two of them already inside her reach.

She got one of them. She got it with the coulter's old curve, which Ketil had not managed
to hammer out and which is still visible in that blade today, and which means the
Ashblade comes off a downstroke a hair wider than it ought to. That hair was what caught
the third one under the jaw.

Then there was the fourth.

·  ·  ·

Bel Brack was on the palisade frame and the wind was toward him and he says she spoke.

He says she was on one knee with her left hand flat on the ash and she was looking up the
slope at the cut bank where the river used to run, and the grass on the bank was lit from
behind because the sun was coming round, and she said that the hillside was worth looking
at.

Six words. Sila cut six words and a number for the fourteenth and I have always assumed
these were the six, though Sila never said so and I never asked her, because she died
before I was born and because I did not become the sort of person who asks such questions
until I was already the fourth hand and there was nobody left to ask.

Nobody else heard it. Nobody else was closer than the fence.

It has been repeated at every wielder's death since. Maren Verren said something about the
quarry face on the eighty-fourth. Colm Grey said nothing that anyone recorded, which the
Hold found so unbearable that it invented something for him within the month and I have
left the invention out. Sable Kettle said it almost word for word, on the hundred and
fifty-fifth, in Wrenfield again, which by then was ash to the ankle and had nothing on it
worth looking at, and she said it anyway, and I do not know whether that was courage or
quotation and I do not think she knew either.

Then she died. That is the flat fact and it wants no help from me.

·  ·  ·

The Hold learned what an Echo was by standing at the fence and watching one be made.

They did not go out. That has been held against them for two hundred days and it will be
held against them after I am finished writing, and the defence, which I find adequate, is
that there were four of the things in the grass and two able-bodied Wardens in the Hold
and one of them was Bran Holt, who was fifty-one and had a bad hip and the tally board.

So they stood at the rails and watched, and what they saw was this.

She did not fall over all the way. She went down onto both knees and then forward onto
her hands and then stopped, in the posture of somebody who is about to get up, and she
stayed in that posture. The body did not settle the way a body settles. The grass around
her lay down in a ring, not flattened but bent, the way grass bends toward water.

By the evening the ring had gone pale at the root. They knew that colour by then. They had
seen it round Oda Ashfall in the same field five days before, and Bran Holt had written it
down, and the word Turning was not in use yet but the thing it names had been noticed and
was being watched.

By the morning of the fifteenth the body was cool and dry and heavier, and the grip had
closed round it, and it was standing.

It had the Ashblade. That is the other thing they learned, and it is the thing that has
governed every decision the Hold has made since: what is out there is not a corpse with a
weapon near it. What is out there has the weapon, and the skill that used the weapon, and
it will have more of both the longer it is left, because the field feeds it.

Two days to a growth. Nobody knew that number on the fifteenth. They found it out on
Maren's schedule, four days late, and it cost her most of a purse and a parry she did not
mean to make. That is the next chapter but two and I am not going to walk into it here.

·  ·  ·

Sila Carver did the arithmetic on the evening of the fourteenth, at the table, with Bran
Holt reading it back to her because she would not look at it twice.

Ten to begin with. That is what any death costs the Hold, and nobody has ever explained
why it is ten and not eight or twelve, and the only defence of the number is that it has
always been right.

Then three for every bond.

Rin Ashfall had five. Her wife. Her mother, five days dead and buried and still walking in
the west field, which is a bond the Hold argued about for a fortnight and then counted
anyway. Ketil Brack, who made her a sword and told her it was not a scythe. Bel Brack,
who was eleven and followed her about and was on the palisade frame because of it. And
Sila Carver herself, who had cut the first pane in the Vale for the girl's mother and now
had to cut the second for the girl.

Five bonds. Fifteen. And ten. Twenty-five.

Sila wrote twenty-five on the board and then wrote, in the margin of the Chronicle in the
cramped abbreviated hand she used to save wax: *the well loved cost more and I have no
argument against the arithmetic and I would like one.*

That line is the first thing any Teller of Ashfall Hold ever wrote that was not a record
of an event, and every Teller since has written something like it, and I am doing it now
in this paragraph, and none of us have found the argument.

Twenty-five of grief on a Hold of twenty-two working people puts the output at something
under nine parts in ten. It is not ruinous. It is the beginning of the sum that ends, at
two hundred, with a settlement working at a third of itself and unable to build its way
out of the hole it is standing in. Bran Holt worked that ceiling out in the following
week, from four numbers and a guess, and he was correct, and the Vale has been steering by
his guess ever since.

·  ·  ·

The Ashblade stayed in the grass for four days.

I mean that literally, and then I mean it the other way. It stayed in the grass because it
was in the grip of the thing that had her, and nobody went out for it. And it stayed in
the grass in the Hold's talk, all through the fifteenth and the sixteenth and the
seventeenth, as a thing lying out there in the weather that belonged to them and that they
were not going to get.

Ketil Brack offered to make another. Maren Verren said no.

That is the whole of the exchange as it comes down to us, and both parts of it are in
character, and Bran Holt did not write down what was said in between, which was a great
deal, and which every subsequent hand has resented him for.

What I have instead is one detail from the Healer, who was in the room: that when Maren
Verren said no, she was holding the cup she had been holding at breakfast on the
fourteenth, and it had been on the table since, and nobody had moved it, because nothing
in the Hold had moved since.
